START HEREHow this works

Ten missions. Each one changes something real in the Storm Deck — the console you just had open. By the end you'll have rewritten most of it, and the version on your screen will be yours, not mine.

Three rules

1 · You cannot break anything permanently

Everything on this drive is a copy. If you wreck a file so badly it won't run, there's a folder called BACKUP — original files — drag the clean copy back out and you're fine. So experiment hard. The kids who learn fastest are the ones who break things on purpose to see what happens.

2 · Read the error, don't fear it

When something goes wrong the Deck shows you a red screen that says exactly what's wrong, in English. That's not failure — that's the computer helping you. Professional programmers see errors dozens of times a day. The difference between them and everyone else is that they read them.

3 · This is real, which means it's sometimes down

You're pulling live data off National Weather Service servers. Sometimes they're slow. Sometimes a weather station goes offline. Sometimes there's nothing happening anywhere and every panel says QUIET. That's not a bug — that's weather. Most of chasing is waiting.

What you need

  • Chrome or Edge — already on your computer.
  • Notepad — already on your computer. Right-click any file → Open with → Notepad.
  • Internet — for live data. The lessons work offline; the Deck doesn't.

That's it. Nothing to install. Nothing to buy. Nobody's permission needed.

One Notepad trick that will save you

When you save, Notepad sometimes tries to add .txt to the end of the filename. If my-settings.js becomes my-settings.js.txt, the Deck won't find it. In the Save box, set Save as type to All Files (*.*). Do that every time and you'll never hit it.

MISSION 01Put yourself on the map

code15 min

Right now the Deck watches Hot Springs, Arkansas — because that's where I set it. It should be watching your town. This is the first line of code you'll ever change, and when you refresh, the map moves to your house.

Do this

Step by step

  1. Go to latlong.net. Type in your town. Write down the two numbers it gives you.
  2. Open the Storm Deck folder. Right-click my-settings.js → Open with → Notepad.
  3. Change lat, lon, townName and state to yours. Put your name in spotterName.
  4. Save. Go to the Deck and press F5.
// before
  lat: 34.5037,
  lon: -93.0552,
  townName: "Hot Springs, AR",
  state: "AR",

// after — this is an example. use YOUR numbers.
  lat: 35.4676,
  lon: -97.5164,
  townName: "Oklahoma City, OK",
  state: "OK",

What you just learned

Those things with names and colons are variables — labeled boxes that hold a value. lat: 34.5037 means "the box called lat holds the number 34.5037."

TypeLooks likeRule
Number34.5037No quotes. Ever.
String (text)"Hot Springs, AR"Always in quotes.
True/falsetrueNo quotes, all lowercase.

Get those three right and you've got maybe a third of what beginners struggle with.

Longitude is negative in America

Longitude measures east–west from a line through Greenwich, England. Everything in the United States is west of it, so every US longitude is a negative number. Type 93.0552 instead of -93.0552 and your map lands in China. The Deck actually checks for this and will tell you.

Now pick your radar

There are 159 NEXRAD radars covering the United States. Each has a four-letter callsign starting with K. Go to radar.weather.gov, click your area, and read the callsign. Put it in radarStation.

Why this matters more than it looks

Radar beams travel in a straight line while the earth curves away underneath. At 60 miles out, the beam is already about 6,000 feet up. So a radar 100 miles away literally cannot see the bottom of a storm — where tornadoes live. Picking the closest radar isn't a preference. It's the difference between seeing low-level rotation and missing it. You'll go deeper on this in Mission 9.

MISSION 02Make it look like yours

code20 min

Code decides what things do. CSS decides what things look like. Open deck.css — the first thing in the file is a list of every color the Deck uses, in one place.

/* deck.css — the top of the file */
:root{
  --bg:      #080b10;   /* page background */
  --panel:   #111721;   /* the boxes */
  --text:    #dbe4ef;   /* normal writing */
  --accent:  #35d6ea;   /* the cyan. change me. */
  --red:     #ff2f45;   /* tornado warning */
}

How a hex color works

#35d6ea is three pairs: 35 red, d6 green, ea blue. Each pair runs 00 (none) to ff (max). So that's a little red, a lot of green, more blue — cyan.

ColorHexRead it as
Pure red#ff0000all red, no green, no blue
White#ffffffall three maxed
Black#000000all three off
Orange#ffa500full red, some green, no blue

Do this

Change --accent to something you like. Save, press F5. Notice how many things changed from that one line — the header, the buttons, the map controls, your location dot. That's the whole point of a variable: define it once, use it everywhere.

Then push it. Make a version so ugly it's funny. Take a screenshot. Then set it back to something you can actually stare at during a warning.

The real lesson

Notice you didn't have to hunt down twelve places where cyan appears. If I'd written #35d6ea in twelve places, you'd have missed some, and the Deck would look half-changed. That's why professionals pull values into one place at the top. It has a name — DRY, Don't Repeat Yourself — and it's one of the few pieces of programming advice that's true everywhere.

MISSION 03See the raw data

codeweather20 min

The Deck is a pretty face on top of plain text that the government publishes for free. Time to look at the actual text. This is the mission that makes the whole thing stop feeling like magic.

Do this

Paste each of these into your browser's address bar. Change AR to your state.

  1. https://api.weather.gov/alerts/active?area=AR
    Every active warning in your state, right now.
  2. https://api.weather.gov/alerts/active?event=Tornado%20Warning
    Every tornado warning in the United States, right now.
  3. https://api.weather.gov/points/34.5037,-93.0552
    Put your own numbers in. Tells you which NWS office covers you.

What you're looking at

That's JSON — the format almost all data on the internet travels in. It's only two ideas, and once you see them you'll see them forever:

// An OBJECT: labeled boxes inside curly braces
{
  "event": "Tornado Warning",
  "severity": "Extreme",
  "areaDesc": "Pima, AZ"
}

// An ARRAY: an ordered list inside square brackets
[ "Tornado Warning", "Severe Thunderstorm Warning", "Flood Warning" ]

// Real NWS data is arrays of objects, nested. That's all it ever is.
{ "features": [ { "properties": { "event": "Tornado Warning" } } ] }

Now go look at line 7 of deck.js. You'll see const NWS = "https://api.weather.gov" — the exact same address you just typed. The Deck isn't doing anything you can't do by hand. It just does it every 60 seconds and draws the result.

Find the polygon

In a tornado warning, scroll to "geometry". That's a list of latitude/longitude pairs — the actual corners of the warned area on the ground. Before 2007 the NWS warned entire counties, so a storm hitting one corner of a county sent everyone in it to the basement. People stopped listening. Now they draw a polygon around the actual threat. Those numbers on your screen are that fix.

MISSION 04Open the hood

code25 min

Every browser has a full set of developer tools built into it, free, that most people never open. Press F12 on the Storm Deck.

Click the Console tab. This is a place you can type JavaScript and it runs instantly. Try these one at a time, pressing Enter after each:

2 + 2

MY.townName

MY.lat

alert("I am inside the Storm Deck")

document.getElementById("threatWord").textContent

That last one reaches into the live page and reads the threat word off the screen. You're not looking at the program from outside anymore. You're standing in it.

Now write a function

Open deck.js in Notepad. Scroll to the very bottom. Add this:

// ── MY CODE ────────────────────────────────────
function stormReport(){
  const dew = document.getElementById("obDew").textContent;
  console.log("Spotter: " + MY.spotterName);
  console.log("Watching: " + MY.townName);
  console.log("Dewpoint: " + dew);

  if (parseInt(dew) >= 65){
    console.log(">> Juicy. There's fuel in the air today.");
  } else {
    console.log(">> Dry. Storms will struggle.");
  }
}

Save, press F5 on the Deck, then type stormReport() in the console and hit Enter.

You just used the three things everything is built from

  • A function — a named bundle of steps you can run whenever you want.
  • A variable — const dew, a box holding a value.
  • A conditional — if / else, the program making a decision.

Functions, variables, and if/else. Every program ever written — games, phones, the software that runs the actual NEXRAD radar — is those three ideas stacked up a very large number of times. You have all three now.

Why 65°F

That number isn't decoration. Dewpoint is how much water vapor the air is actually holding, and water vapor is the fuel a thunderstorm burns. Below about 55°F, severe storms struggle. At 65°F and up in spring, the atmosphere is loaded. Forecasters watch the dewpoint line on a surface map the way you'd watch a gas gauge.

MISSION 05Teach it a new alert

codeweather25 min

The NWS issues over 120 different kinds of alerts. The Deck only sorts a handful. Time to teach it more.

Find this in deck.js (section 6):

function classify(eventName){
  const e = eventName.toLowerCase();
  if (e.includes("tornado") && e.includes("warning"))  return "tornado";
  if (e.includes("extreme wind"))                       return "tornado";
  if (e.includes("severe thunderstorm") && e.includes("warning")) return "severe";
  if (e.includes("flash flood") || e.includes("flood")) return "flood";
  if (e.includes("watch"))                              return "watch";
  return "other";
}

Read it out loud

toLowerCase() makes everything lowercase first, so "Tornado Warning" and "TORNADO WARNING" both match. includes() asks "is this word anywhere in there?" && means AND — both must be true. || means OR — either will do.

Order matters, and here's the trap

JavaScript checks these top to bottom and stops at the first match. The "watch" line is near the bottom on purpose. If you moved it to the top, a Tornado Watch would match "watch" first and get filed as a routine watch — and so would a Tornado Warning... no wait. Read it again. Would it? Check the actual words. This is the kind of thing you have to slow down and trace by hand, and it's exactly where real bugs live.

Add winter weather

Add a line — above the "watch" line — that catches blizzards and ice storms:

  if (e.includes("blizzard") || e.includes("ice storm") ||
      e.includes("winter storm")) return "winter";

Then you need to tell the rest of the program what "winter" is worth and what color it is. Find POINTS and COLORS just below and add your new kind to both:

const POINTS = { tornado:100, severe:65, flood:45, winter:50, watch:35, other:15 };

const COLORS = {
  tornado:"#ff2f45", severe:"#ffb020", flood:"#35ea7a",
  winter:"#a8d8ff", watch:"#b46bff", other:"#7b8ba0"
};

And one more spot — deck.css needs to know how to style it. Copy the .warn.flood block, rename it .warn.winter, and change the color.

Notice what just happened

One new feature, four files-worth of places to touch. That's normal, and it's why programmers care so much about keeping related things together. When you find yourself thinking "ugh, I have to change it in four places" — that feeling is real, it's called coupling, and learning to reduce it is most of what separates a beginner from a professional.

MISSION 06Your own red list

code25 min

In my-settings.js there's a list called redAlerts that nothing currently reads. It's a loaded gun with no trigger. Let's wire it up.

  redAlerts: [
    "Tornado Warning",
    "Particularly Dangerous Situation",
    "Extreme Wind Warning",
    "Flash Flood Emergency",
  ],

That's an array — an ordered list. You get at items by number, and counting starts at zero, which trips up everyone at first:

MY.redAlerts[0]      // "Tornado Warning"
MY.redAlerts[1]      // "Particularly Dangerous Situation"
MY.redAlerts.length  // 4
MY.redAlerts[4]      // undefined — there is no 5th item

Try those in the console (F12) right now.

Wire it in

In deck.js, inside classify(), add this as the very first check inside the function — before all the other ifs:

  // My own list from my-settings.js wins over everything else.
  const isMine = MY.redAlerts.some(function(name){
    return e.includes(name.toLowerCase());
  });
  if (isMine) return "tornado";

Now add something to redAlerts that actually happens where you live — "Severe Thunderstorm Warning" if you want every one of those screaming at you, or "Tornado Watch" if you want to know the moment the day turns. Save, F5.

What .some() does

.some() walks the whole list and asks your question about each item. If the answer is yes for any of them, it hands back true. There's a matching .every() that only says true if all of them pass. You'll use both for the rest of your life.

The alternative is writing a loop yourself, which works fine and which you should try once so you know what .some() is doing for you.

MISSION 07Build a better score

codeweather40 min

The threat meter runs on a rule I made up: whatever the worst active alert is, that's the score. It's crude. It ignores the atmosphere entirely. You can do better, and this is where you stop copying code and start designing it.

What my version misses

  • Five severe warnings at once is worse than one. Mine scores them the same.
  • A warning 200 miles away counts as much as one over your roof.
  • A 78°F dewpoint day and a 45°F dewpoint day score identically.
  • A Day-1 Enhanced SPC risk doesn't move the needle at all.

Your job

Rewrite scoreThreat() so it uses more than one input. A starting shape — the numbers are yours to argue with:

  let score = 5;

  // 1. worst active alert (what mine already does)
  alerts.forEach(function(a){
    const kind = classify(a.properties.event);
    if (POINTS[kind] > score) score = POINTS[kind];
  });

  // 2. NEW: a crowded sky is a worse sky
  score = score + (alerts.length * 3);

  // 3. NEW: moisture. Fuel in the air.
  const dew = parseInt(document.getElementById("obDew").textContent);
  if (dew >= 70)      score = score + 15;
  else if (dew >= 65) score = score + 10;
  else if (dew < 55)  score = score - 10;

  // 4. never let it run off the ends
  if (score > 100) score = 100;
  if (score < 0)   score = 0;

Careful — a real bug lives here

If the weather station is offline, obDew reads —, and parseInt("—") gives you NaN — "Not a Number". Then score + NaN is NaN, and your meter shows nothing at all, with no error message. This is a genuinely nasty class of bug: it fails silently and looks like the program just stopped caring.

Guard it:

  if (!isNaN(dew)){
    // ... only do the dewpoint math when we really got a number
  }

Get in the habit now: whenever you convert text into a number, ask what happens if the text wasn't a number.

This is the actual job

What you're doing right now — deciding which ingredients matter and how much to weight each one — is a simplified version of what severe weather forecasting is. Real forecasters combine instability, moisture, lift and wind shear into composite indices with names like the Significant Tornado Parameter. Same idea: several measurements, weighted, into one number that helps you decide.

Yours will be worse than theirs. Theirs was worse than this once too.

MISSION 08The chase log

codefield40 min

Every serious spotter keeps a log. What you saw, when, where, and what the radar looked like at that moment. Yours is going to save itself in the browser so it's still there tomorrow.

The tool that remembers

localStorage is a small box the browser keeps on your computer. Whatever you put in it is still there after you close the window, restart, or shut down for a week. Try it in the console:

localStorage.setItem("test", "hello");
localStorage.getItem("test");   // "hello"

Now close the whole browser. Open it again. Run getItem again. Still there.

It only stores text

localStorage can't hold an object — only a string. So you turn your object into text going in, and back into an object coming out:

JSON.stringify(obj)   // object  →  text, for saving
JSON.parse(text)      // text  →  object, for reading

Forgetting one of those two is one of the most common beginner bugs there is. You'll do it. Then you'll remember this box.

Build it

Add to the bottom of deck.js:

function logIt(what){
  const entries = JSON.parse(localStorage.getItem("chaseLog") || "[]");

  entries.push({
    when:  new Date().toISOString(),
    where: MY.townName,
    what:  what,
    temp:  document.getElementById("obTemp").textContent,
    dew:   document.getElementById("obDew").textContent,
    threat:document.getElementById("threatWord").textContent
  });

  localStorage.setItem("chaseLog", JSON.stringify(entries));
  console.log("Logged. You now have " + entries.length + " entries.");
}

function showLog(){
  console.table(JSON.parse(localStorage.getItem("chaseLog") || "[]"));
}

Save, F5, then in the console:

logIt("First entry. Clear sky, learning the system.");
showLog();

console.table() prints it as a real table. Nice touch nobody tells beginners about.

Why || "[]" matters

The very first time this runs, there's no chaseLog yet, so getItem returns null — and JSON.parse(null) would crash. The || "[]" means "or, if that's empty, use an empty list instead." Handling the first run, when nothing exists yet, is a case beginners forget constantly and it crashes on the one person who matters: the first user.

Then make it real

Log every storm you watch this season, even boring ones. Especially boring ones — that's how you learn what normal looks like, which is the only way to recognize abnormal. In Mission 10 you'll put it on the screen properly.

MISSION 09Hunt the hook echo

weatherfieldongoing

No code in this one. This is the skill the code exists to serve. You're going to learn to look at a radar image and know what's about to happen.

The thing you're looking for

A supercell's mesocyclone — its rotating updraft — drags precipitation around itself in a curl. On radar that curl shows up as a hook on the back-right side of the storm. Underneath the notch of that hook is where tornadoes form.

When you hear that a warning is "radar-indicated," this is usually what somebody saw.

Practice without waiting for a storm

The National Weather Service archives every radar scan ever taken. You can replay the worst days in American history, frame by frame, tonight:

mesonet.agron.iastate.edu — RADAR + warnings archive

Days worth studying — look them up and step through the loop:

  • May 3, 1999 — Oklahoma City. The Bridge Creek–Moore F5.
  • April 27, 2011 — the Super Outbreak. 360 tornadoes in 24 hours.
  • May 22, 2011 — Joplin, Missouri.
  • May 20, 2013 — Moore, Oklahoma.

Watch what the storm looked like 30 minutes before anything happened. That's the part that matters. Anyone can spot a tornado on radar once it's on the ground.

Wall cloud or shelf cloud?

This is the single most common mistake new spotters make, and it's the one that puts a wrong report on the air.

Wall cloudShelf cloud
WhereUnder the rain-free base, back of the stormLeading edge, front of the storm
AirFlowing into itFlowing out of it
MotionMay rotateRolls, doesn't rotate
Then whatCan produce a tornadoGust front. Wind, then rain.

The tell is the wind on your face. If wind is rushing toward the cloud, it's inflow — that's a wall cloud, pay attention. If wind is blasting out at you and the temperature just dropped, that's outflow — shelf cloud. The storm is exhaling, not inhaling.

The part radar can't do

Radar sees rain and wind. It cannot see a tornado. Even a textbook hook echo with tight rotation only tells you a tornado is likely — which is why NWS warnings say "radar-indicated."

The exception is the tornado debris signature: modern dual-polarization radar can detect that the stuff in the air is no longer raindrops — it's splinters, insulation, dirt. When forecasters see a TDS, they know something is on the ground tearing up houses. That's when warning language escalates.

And that is exactly why trained human spotters still exist in the age of radar. The Field Manual in this lab goes deeper.

MISSION 10Capstone — your own panel

codeas long as it takes

No instructions this time. You know enough now. Add something to the Storm Deck that isn't there, that you want, that nobody told you to build.

How to add a panel

Three files, in this order — this is the pattern for every feature you'll ever add:

  1. deck.html — make the empty box exist. Copy an existing <div class="panel">, give its inner div a new id.
  2. deck.js — write a function that goes and gets the data, then puts it into that id with .innerHTML.
  3. deck.js again — call your function from inside refresh() so it updates on the timer with everything else.

Ideas, roughly easiest first

IdeaWhat you'd learn
Your chase log, on screen instead of in the consoleBuilding HTML from an array
A countdown to when the nearest warning expiresWorking with dates and time
Distance from you to each warning, in milesReal math on a sphere
The 7-day forecast from /gridpointsReading unfamiliar API docs
A "chase day rating" from 0–10 with your reasoning shownDesign, not just code
A dark/light toggle that remembers your choiceCSS classes + localStorage
Sound a different tone per alert typeThe Web Audio API

The distance one is worth doing

Getting distance between two lat/lon points takes the haversine formula — real spherical trigonometry, because the earth is round and flat math lies at long distances. You'll find it in thirty seconds online. Using math you haven't formally learned yet, to solve a problem you actually have, is the single fastest way to get good at math. Your teachers would say the same thing; this time you'll believe it because you needed it.

When it works, do this

Screenshot it. Write down what it does and what fought you. Keep that.

In four years, a college application or a job interview is going to ask what you've built. "I built a live severe-weather console with NEXRAD radar overlay and NWS warning polygons, and here's the part I designed myself" — at thirteen — is not a normal answer.

AFTER THE MISSIONSWhere this actually goes

You've written JavaScript, read live government data feeds, and learned to read a radar. Here's the honest map of what's next.

Get certified — this year

The SKYWARN folder on this drive is the real path. NWS spotter training is free, and it makes you a trained spotter whose reports go to actual National Weather Service forecasters. Most people who say they're into storms never do this. Do it, and you're not a kid with a weather app anymore.

Learn Python next

JavaScript is what the web runs on. Python is what meteorology runs on. The real tools — MetPy for calculations, Py-ART for raw radar — are Python, and they read the same NEXRAD Level II files the NWS works from. Everything you learned here transfers; the ideas are identical and only the punctuation changes.

The school part, said plainly

Nearly every professional storm chaser is a meteorologist, a researcher, or a broadcaster who chases as part of the job. That path runs through a math and physics degree. Atmospheric science is calculus and fluid dynamics wearing a raincoat.

So the most useful thing you can do at thirteen isn't more radar. It's taking the hardest math your school offers, every single year, without skipping one. Everything else here is easier than that and matters less.

The whole roadmap is written out for you

There's a page on this drive called The Long Game. It has the real numbers — how many of these jobs exist, what they pay, the exact college coursework the federal government requires, which summer camps and competitions will take someone your age this year, and which famous ones won't. It's blunt. Read it once, then get back to the storms.

The rule that doesn't bend

You don't chase

Not yet. Not alone. Not with friends. Not "just to the edge of town."

Storm chasing kills experienced adults with degrees and decades of experience. On May 31, 2013 in El Reno, Oklahoma, it killed Tim Samaras — one of the most careful, respected researchers in the field — along with his son Paul and his partner Carl Young. That storm did something no one predicted.

You spot from home. You watch radar, you watch the sky from your own yard, you report what you see. That's real spotting, it's what the NWS actually wants, and it's how every good chaser started. The driving comes later, with training, with an experienced adult, in a vehicle with a real escape route.

The best chasers are old. That's not an accident — it's the whole point.
