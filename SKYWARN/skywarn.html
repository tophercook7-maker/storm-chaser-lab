REAL CREDENTIALS · FREE Get certified by the National Weather Service.

There is an actual program, run by the actual federal government, that trains ordinary people to watch storms and report what they see — and the National Weather Service uses those reports to decide whether to issue a warning. It's called SKYWARN. It costs nothing. This page is the whole path, start to finish.

Looking up your local National Weather Service office…

What SKYWARN actually is

The National Weather Service has radar. Radar is incredible — you're looking at it in the Storm Deck right now. But radar has a hard limit: it tells you what's happening thousands of feet in the air, not what's happening on the ground.

Radar can show a tight rotation. It cannot tell you whether that rotation has put a tornado on the ground, whether the hail is the size of a pea or a baseball, or whether a road is under water. For that, the NWS needs people. Real people, standing in real places, looking out real windows.

That's SKYWARN. It started in the 1960s and 70s, and today there are between 350,000 and 400,000 trained spotters in the United States.

The phrase to remember

NWS calls spotter reports "ground truth." Everything else — radar, satellite, computer models — is an inference. A spotter report is an observation. When a warning meteorologist has radar suggesting something and a trained spotter confirming it, that's when the warning goes out fast and confident.

When you get certified, you're not getting a participation trophy. You're getting added to the list of people whose phone call changes what the government does.

A spotter is not a chaser. Know the difference cold.

This is the single most misunderstood thing about this whole field, and the NWS is blunt about it:

Storm spotterStorm chaser
Stays close to home. Reports the weather that comes to them. Drives toward the storm, often hundreds of miles.
Trained and certified by the National Weather Service. No license, no certification, no requirement of any kind.
Usually tied to a local agency, fire department, or radio net. Usually on their own, or with a tour company.
The NWS asks for and uses their reports. The NWS uses good chaser reports too — but never asks anyone to go get them.

Directly from NWS guidance

Spotters "will never be instructed to drive somewhere else to look for bad weather" — reports come from home locations. Whether you are mobile or at a fixed location, safety is a storm spotter's number one priority. Spotters are not expected to risk their safety, and are told to avoid speculation when they report.

Read that again. The federal agency that wants your reports is telling you it will never ask you to go anywhere dangerous to get them. That's not the NWS being cautious with a kid. That's what they tell every spotter in the country, including the sixty-year-old fire chiefs.

You are going to be a spotter. Spotting is the real credential, it's what the NWS actually needs, and it's how essentially every good chaser started. The driving comes later — years later, with training, with an experienced adult, and with a vehicle you're old enough to drive.

The age question — the honest answer

You're going to hear three different answers to "how old do I have to be," because there is no single national age rule. Each NWS forecast office sets its own policy. Here's what's actually true:

  • Some offices require 18. The Baltimore/Washington office and the Philadelphia/Mt Holly office both state you must be at least 18 to become a spotter.
  • Some local SKYWARN groups certify at 14.
  • Many offices say the same thing: because severe storms are complex and dangerous, spotting is recommended for adults 18 and older — but high school and middle school students are welcome to attend the classes with a parent or another adult.
  • Many offices — including NWS Little Rock — publish no age requirement at all.

So here's your move

Do not guess, and do not let anyone guess for you. Call your office and ask. Have an adult with you when you call, or have them make the call. The question is one sentence:

"My son is 13 and wants to take the spotter class. What's your office's policy on age, and can he attend with me?"

Read this part carefully

If the answer is "he can take the class but we can't certify him until he's 18" — take the class anyway. Everything in it is real training. Nothing about it expires from your head. And there are two other programs on this page (CoCoRaHS and ham radio) with no age barrier at all, where your reports go to the NWS starting the day you sign up.

Getting the certificate

This part is different at every office, so here's exactly how it works at NWS Little Rock (LZK) — and the shape is similar most places.

When you finish the online course, there's a box to check saying you want to be an active spotter. Then:

  • Around the 1st and the 15th of each month, the Little Rock office receives a list of everyone who completed the course in the previous half-month and asked to be an active spotter.
  • The Warning Coordination Meteorologist emails out certificates, along with spotting reminders and the toll-free severe weather reporting number.
  • The certificate makes you an official NWS storm spotter for five years.

One local quirk worth knowing

NWS Little Rock states plainly: "our office does not assign spotter numbers." Some offices issue you an ID number; Little Rock doesn't. So if a form asks for your spotter ID and you're an LZK spotter, the honest answer is that your office doesn't issue them — you give your name and location instead. Don't invent a number. Ever. Your credibility as a spotter is the entire thing you have.

Go in person if you possibly can

The in-person class is free, takes about two hours, and is usually held in the late winter and spring before severe season. It's taught by an actual NWS meteorologist, in a room, who will answer your questions. Going to that class and asking a good question is worth more to your future than any ten hours you'll spend online. Check the schedule page in January and February.

What you report — and what you don't

Spotters don't report weather. Spotters report severe weather. If you call in every rainstorm, people stop picking up. Here's the actual bar:

Report thisThreshold
Tornado or funnel cloudAny. No size threshold. Report it immediately.
HailQuarter size — 1.00 inch — or larger. That's the legal severe threshold.
Wind58 mph or greater, or wind damage you can describe.
FloodingWater over a road, in a structure, or rising fast.
Wall cloudEspecially if it is rotating or persistent.
DamageTrees down, power lines down, roof damage — say what and where.

How to give a report

Four things, in this order, every time:

  1. What you saw — using the right word. "Wall cloud, rotating" is a different report from "shelf cloud."
  2. Where — distance and direction from a town, plus the county. Not "near my house." "Three miles south of Hot Springs, Garland County."
  3. When — the actual time you saw it, not the time you're calling.
  4. Who — your name, and that you're a trained spotter.

The rule that makes you trusted

If you aren't sure, say you aren't sure. "I have a lowering that might be a wall cloud, I can't confirm rotation" is a genuinely useful report. Guessing and being wrong once costs you more than staying quiet ten times. The NWS trains spotters to avoid speculation for exactly this reason.

The Chase Log in this lab is built around this format on purpose — it's already asking you for what, where, when, and it stamps the conditions. Practice in the log now and the real report will come out clean when it counts.

CoCoRaHS — the one you can join today

While you're sorting out the SKYWARN age question, there's a second national volunteer network you can join right now with no age requirement whatsoever. It's called CoCoRaHS — the Community Collaborative Rain, Hail and Snow Network.

You measure precipitation where you live and report it. The National Weather Service uses that data — it's how they see where the rain actually fell, at a resolution radar can't give them. Their own stated position: there is no age requirement, as long as you can report routinely.

CostThe official 4-inch rain gauge runs $42–$50 through the CoCoRaHS store. Nothing else. (If you see "$35" quoted somewhere online, that's an old price — check the store, not a blog.)
TimeAbout 60 seconds a day, at roughly the same time each morning.
Sign upcocorahs.org/application.aspx

Why this matters more than it sounds

CoCoRaHS gives you the single most underrated skill in this entire field: the habit of daily observation. Measuring nothing on a dry Tuesday is not a wasted day — it's how you build the instinct for what normal looks like. And a kid with three years of unbroken CoCoRaHS reports has something on paper that no essay can fake.

Ham radio — how spotters actually talk, and the $5 way in

When severe weather hits, cell networks get congested and sometimes fail. Amateur radio doesn't depend on any of that infrastructure. That's why the SKYWARN net in Arkansas is run by licensed ham operators, straight into the NWS office.

You do not need a license to be a SKYWARN spotter — a telephone is fine, and the NWS says so explicitly. But the license is the door into the operational side of this, and here's the part almost nobody knows:

Under 18, the license is essentially free

There are two routes, and one of them is cheaper than the one everybody quotes:

  • ARRL VEC route: exam fee for candidates under 18 is $5 (regular fee is up to $15). The one-time $35 FCC application fee is reimbursed by the ARRL Youth Licensing Grant Program, in effect since April 2022. You pay the FCC, then ARRL mails the money back. Net cost: about $5.
  • GLAARG VEC route: the Greater Los Angeles Amateur Radio Group waives the exam fee entirely for anyone 18 or under — and they run online sessions you can take from home. glaarg.org  ·  sessions at hamstudy.org/sessions/online. Net cost: $0 for the exam (you still owe the FCC its $35, and the ARRL youth grant is tied to ARRL VEC, so weigh that).

Either way, a federal radio license costs you somewhere between nothing and five dollars. That is not a typo.

The Technician exam

  • Element 2 (Technician) — 35 multiple-choice questions, 26 correct to pass.
  • Every possible question is published in advance. The pool in effect from July 1, 2026 through June 30, 2030 has 409 questions. You can study the literal exam.
  • Free study tools exist — practice exams that drill the actual pool until you're passing consistently. Don't schedule the test until you're scoring 85%+ on practice runs.

What you have to do before exam day

This trips people up — do it in advance

You are required to register in the FCC's CORES system and get an FRN (FCC Registration Number) before exam day. apps.fcc.gov/cores — this requires a Social Security Number, so an adult must do this part with you.

ID for minors: if you don't have a photo ID, you need only one identification item if a legal guardian presents their photo ID. Otherwise, two non-photo IDs — a birth certificate, school ID, or library card all count. Bring proof you're under 18 or you'll be charged the full fee.

Find a session (in person or online): home.arrl.org/find-an-amateur-radio-license-exam-session  ·  Youth grant details: arrl.org/youth-licensing-grant-program

If you're near Hot Springs, your test session is basically down the road

The Spa Amateur Radio Association (WB5SPA) runs VE test sessions locally. They meet monthly on Tuesdays at 6:00 PM at the Garland County Department of Emergency Management, 401 Mid America Blvd, Hot Springs. wb5spa.org

Read that address again. The place where you'd take your radio exam is the county's emergency management building — the same building that cares a great deal when a warning goes out. Two other clubs are nearby too: the Hot Springs ARC (146.88, Thursdays 7 PM) and the Garland ARC. Have an adult email or call ahead before showing up; club schedules drift.

Closer to home: Diamond Lakes Area Skywarn

That statewide net runs out of North Little Rock. But Hot Springs and Garland County have their own Skywarn group — Diamond Lakes Area Skywarn — and these are the frequencies that matter most where you actually live:

Main146.76−, PL tone 114.8
Also used147.015+  ·  146.715−

Program these three first

A $25 handheld radio, three frequencies programmed in, and you can listen today — no license needed to receive. Put 146.76 (tone 114.8) in slot one and 162.550 in slot two. One of them tells you what the National Weather Service is saying. The other tells you what people standing outside in it are saying. Hearing both at once is the whole education.

Listen before you ever transmit

You can listen to a repeater with a cheap handheld radio before you're licensed — receiving is legal for anyone. Listen to that monthly training net. Listen to how a real report sounds: short, calm, formatted, no drama. That's the voice you want to have the first time you key up.

SKYWARN Recognition Day

Established in 1999 by the NWS and the ARRL, held the first Saturday in December. NWS offices open their doors and operate amateur radio stations, contacting spotters all over the country. It's a public event at a real forecast office. Go to it. It is the single easiest way for a kid to end up standing in an NWS operations center talking to actual meteorologists, and it happens once a year.

What you'll actually have

Twelve months from now, if you do the list above, you are:

  • A trained NWS storm spotter — or first in line to be one the day your office allows it.
  • A federally licensed radio operator with your own callsign.
  • A CoCoRaHS observer with a year of unbroken daily data in a national database.
  • Someone with a logbook of storms he watched, described, and got right.
  • Someone who can read radar and write code that reads it for him.

You'd be thirteen or fourteen years old. There are working adults in weather-adjacent jobs who don't have that list.

And the rule that doesn't bend

None of this involves driving toward a storm. Not one item. The National Weather Service — the people who want your reports — will never ask you to.

Storm chasing kills experienced adults with degrees and decades in the field. Read the last page of the Missions if you haven't. You spot from safety. That's not the junior version of this. That's the job.

Four things the internet will tell you that are wrong

  • "skywarn.org is the official site." It is not. It has no connection to the National Weather Service, and as of this writing it serves gambling content. The official pages live on weather.gov. Don't cite it, don't sign up on it, don't send anyone there.
  • "Become a Weather-Ready Nation Ambassador." That program accepts organizations only — businesses, schools, clubs. Individuals of any age cannot join. Half the "how to get started in weather" lists online get this wrong.
  • "Volunteer at NSSL." The National Severe Storms Laboratory states plainly that legal liability issues prevent NSSL from accepting volunteers. Don't waste an email on it.
  • "Spotter classes run year-round." They cluster February through spring, before severe season. If your office's class page looks empty in August, that's normal — check again in January.
