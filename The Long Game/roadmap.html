THE LONG GAME How you actually turn this into a life.

This is the page nobody writes for a thirteen-year-old, because it involves telling you some things you'd rather not hear. Read it anyway. Every number here comes from the federal government or from the labs themselves — and the reason it's worth reading is that the honest version turns out to have more doors in it than the fantasy version, not fewer.

1 · The honest version

If you tell an adult you want to be a storm chaser, one of two things happens. They either smile and say "that's great!" and change the subject, or they tell you to be realistic and pick something else. Both of those are useless to you.

Here's the third answer, which is the true one: "storm chaser" is not a job. But almost every skill a storm chaser has is attached to a job — several of them — and you're old enough to start stacking those skills right now.

What this page is for

Not to talk you out of anything. To make sure that when you're seventeen and filling out applications, you already have the coursework, the credentials and the logbook that the fourteen-year-old version of you didn't know were required. That's the entire difference between people who end up in this field and people who end up telling stories about how they almost did.

2 · There is no "storm chaser" job

This isn't an opinion. It's a direct quote from the National Severe Storms Laboratory — the federal lab in Norman, Oklahoma that does the actual field research, the one you've seen in documentaries:

"There is no 'Storm Chaser' job description at NSSL."

NSSL · faq

They go further. When people ask how to work there:

"Nearly all of our meteorologists at the lab have master's degrees or PhDs."

NSSL

And the Storm Prediction Center — the office that issues every tornado watch in the country — says this about chasing itself:

"The National Weather Service does not endorse storm chasing because of the risk, but welcomes storm reports from those who do chase."

"After gaining experience observing storms as a spotter, you can then decide if chasing is for you."

NOAA Storm Prediction Center · Tornado FAQ

Read that second quote again — it's a recipe

The Storm Prediction Center just told you the order of operations, in writing. Spotter first. Then decide. That's not them being cautious with a kid — that's the sequence they'd give an adult. And the spotter half is free, safe, and available to you immediately. It's the SKYWARN page.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics tracks every occupation in the country. Here's atmospheric scientists, from the May 2024 data:

Nine thousand four hundred. That's smaller than a lot of high schools' graduating classes added together across one state. This is a small, competitive field, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something.

And here's the part that surprises people

Where they workShare of jobsMedian pay

The glamorous job pays the worst

The one everybody pictures — on TV, in front of the green screen, name recognition — pays roughly $45,000 a year less than the quiet federal job where you sit in an operations center and decide whether to put a tornado warning on 40,000 people's phones. It's also the smaller half of the field. Worth knowing before you aim.

Now here's why none of that should discourage you: 700 openings a year is not zero. It's 700 people, every single year, who got there. The bar isn't luck. The bar is a very specific list of coursework, and you're about to see it.

4 · What the federal job actually requires

Federal jobs have published, exact requirements. The meteorologist series is OPM GS-1340, and this is the whole gate. Not a vibe. A list:

RequirementHours
Meteorology coursework, total24 semester hrs
  — of which: dynamic meteorology / thermodynamics≥ 6 hrs
  — of which: synoptic / mesoscale meteorology≥ 6 hrs
  — of which: physical meteorology≥ 3 hrs
  — of which: remote sensing / instrumentation≥ 2 hrs
  — of which: electives9 hrs
Physics, with laboratory6 hrs
Ordinary differential equations3 hrs

Computer science is explicitly on the approved elective list

Read that again, because it changes what you're doing on this flash drive. The coding in the Missions is not a hobby you're doing instead of weather. Under the federal standard, it counts toward the degree. You are already, at thirteen, working on the elective column of a federal job requirement.

The one that decides everything

Ordinary differential equations

See that line? To take differential equations you need calculus. To take dynamic meteorology and thermodynamics — the two biggest blocks on the list — you need calculus. To take physics with lab, you need calculus.

Three of the four requirements are locked behind math. Not behind talent, not behind knowing storms, not behind how many videos you've watched. Behind math class. The students who don't make it into this field overwhelmingly don't fail out of meteorology — they arrive at college without the math to start it.

So if you want one sentence out of this whole page, here it is: take the hardest math available to you, every single year, starting now. That's the fast track. It's not glamorous and it's not what you wanted the answer to be, and it happens to be true.

5 · What that means for you right now, at 13

You're in middle school. Here is the entire strategy, in four lines:

The whole plan fits on a napkin

  1. Math track. Get into the most advanced math sequence your school offers. If there's an algebra placement test in 8th grade, that test matters more to your career than any weather thing on this drive. Aim to be taking calculus by senior year.
  2. Keep coding. Finish the Missions, then keep going. It's a federal elective and it's also how modern meteorology is actually done.
  3. Build the record. SKYWARN, CoCoRaHS, the Chase Log. Years of daily data and real reports is a thing almost no applicant has.
  4. Get in the room. Camps, competitions, SKYWARN Recognition Day. Meet people who do this for a living, while you're still young enough that they find it charming.

6 · What's actually open to you at 13

Most "get started in weather" lists online are written for college students and quietly waste your time. These are the ones that will genuinely take someone your age.

Summer camps

ProgramWhoCost
Penn State Weather CampEntering grades 8–10$1,050
Blue Hill Observatory (Massachusetts)Rising 8th grade$850, scholarships available
OU Project Crossroads (Norman, OK)Rising 9th grade — one year out$260

Register in winter or you'll miss it

Weather camp registration closes in spring, months before the camp runs — Penn State's 2026 sessions closed on May 16. If you go looking in June, they're full. Put a reminder on a calendar for January. Ask about scholarships; Blue Hill has them and most people never ask.

OU Project Crossroads is the one to circle. It's $260, it's in Norman, Oklahoma — inside the National Weather Center, the same building as NSSL and the Storm Prediction Center — and it starts at rising 9th grade. That's your next-year target, and it's about a four-and-a-half hour drive from Arkansas.

Competitions you're the right age for

CompetitionWhoWhen to apply
Thermo Fisher Scientific Junior Innovators ChallengeGrades 6–8Feb 1 – Jun 10
Science Olympiad — "Everyday Weather"Division B = middle schoolThrough your school

Two things the internet gets wrong about these

The Junior Innovators Challenge used to be called Broadcom MASTERS — that name no longer exists, and searching for it gets you dead pages. And Science Olympiad's meteorology event is a Division B event, so it's open to middle schoolers; a lot of lists wrongly file it under high school only. Ask a science teacher whether your school has a Science Olympiad team. If it doesn't, ask what it would take to start one — that question alone has launched more science careers than most classes.

And the free ones, today

  • SKYWARN training — free, online, and you can do it tonight. See the SKYWARN page.
  • CoCoRaHS — no age requirement at all. Start the daily-observation habit now, and by the time you're applying to college you'll have years of it.
  • SKYWARN Recognition Day, first Saturday in December — NWS offices open their doors. This is the single easiest way for a kid to end up standing in an operations center talking to working meteorologists.

A trick worth learning early

Every single one of those was checked by going to the program's own page and reading the eligibility line — not by trusting a listicle. That habit is worth more to you over the next ten years than any fact on this drive. Go to the source. The source is usually right there.

8 · Where you'd actually go to school

No Arkansas university offers a meteorology degree

There is no atmospheric science or meteorology bachelor's program in the state. This is worth knowing at thirteen instead of at seventeen, because it means the plan involves leaving — and that's a conversation to start having with your parents early and calmly, not a surprise to spring on them during senior year.

The realistic options:

SchoolWhyDistance
University of Oklahoma (Norman)The meteorology program is housed inside the National Weather Center — the same building as NSSL and the Storm Prediction Center. Students walk past working severe-weather forecasters to get to class.~4.5 hrs
University of Louisiana at MonroeHas its own polarimetric S-band radar on campus. Undergraduates operating real research radar is rare.~4 hrs
Mississippi State UniversityOffers a fully online BS in geosciences/meteorology — at in-state tuition rates for everyone, regardless of where you live. The route that doesn't require moving.online

Two corrections to the list you'll see online

Colorado State gets recommended constantly and has no undergraduate meteorology major — it's a graduate department. Texas Tech has only a minor at the undergraduate level. Both are excellent places to go for a master's later; neither is where you go at eighteen. Check the actual degree catalog before you fall in love with a school.

9 · The five-year map

This month · age 13

SKYWARN training + CoCoRaHS + start the log

Free, immediate, and all three build records that compound. The daily habit is the point.

This year · age 13

Get on the most advanced math track your school has

Talk to a counselor. Say the words "I want to be taking calculus by senior year, what do I have to do now?" Adults respond very differently to a specific question than to a dream.

This year

Ham Technician license + Science Olympiad

Costs $0–$5 under 18. A federal license and a competition record, in one school year.

Next January

Apply to a weather camp — register early

Penn State or Blue Hill now; OU Project Crossroads the year you're a rising 9th grader. Ask about scholarships.

Age 14–15 · high school

Physics, AP math, and keep coding

Every one of these is on the GS-1340 list. You're not preparing for the requirement — you're already doing it.

Age 16–17

Visit Norman. Tour the National Weather Center.

Walk the building. Standing in a place makes it real in a way no website does — and it turns "someday" into a campus you've already been to.

Age 18

Enroll — OU, ULM, or Mississippi State online

With calculus already behind you, a decade of storm logs, a spotter certificate, a callsign, and code you wrote yourself.

What you'd be walking in with

Do the list above and at eighteen you are an applicant with: a federal spotter certification, a federal radio license, five years of unbroken CoCoRaHS observations in a national database, a personal storm log, working code, and the math to start the major on day one.

Roughly 700 people a year get one of these jobs. That version of you is a serious candidate for one of them. Not because of talent. Because at thirteen you knew what the list was.

The rule that outlasts all of this

Everything on this page is about the next ten years. This part is about all of them.

Not one single item above requires you to drive toward a storm. Not the certification, not the license, not the data, not the degree, not the job. The people who issue the warnings — the ones you want to work with — will never ask you to. Read the last section of the Field Manual and remember what happened at El Reno to a man who was better at this than any of us. You spot from safety. That's not the junior version of this job. That is the job.
