DECODEReading a METAR

A METAR is the standard weather report every airport on Earth puts out, usually once an hour. It looks like a license plate that fell in a blender. Once you can read it, you can pull the current conditions for almost any spot on the planet from one short line of text — no app, no login. This page teaches the code, then lets you decode real ones right here.

1 · What a METAR is (and why chasers read it)

METAR stands for METeorological Aerodrome Report (it's a French acronym — the format is international). It's a surface observation taken at an airport, mostly by an automated station (called an ASOS or AWOS) with a human sometimes editing it. Airports report because pilots need to know the wind, visibility, and clouds before they land. That same data is gold for storm chasers.

Why you care

A METAR gives you the real, measured air right now at a point on the ground: wind direction and speed, gusts, how far you can see, cloud bases and cover, temperature, and — the big one — dewpoint. Dewpoint is your fuel gauge for storms. Radar shows you the storm; the METAR network shows you the environment the next storm has to grow in.

A typical METAR looks like this:

KHOT 041853Z 18012G22KT 10SM FEW055 SCT250 34/23 A2996 RMK AO2

That one line means: Hot Springs, Arkansas airport, reported on the 4th at 18:53 UTC. Wind from the south at 12 knots gusting 22. Visibility 10 miles. A few clouds at 5,500 ft, scattered at 25,000 ft. Temperature 34 °C, dewpoint 23 °C. Altimeter 29.96 inches. Automated station with a precipitation sensor. Every one of those pieces has a fixed spot and a fixed code, which is exactly why a computer — or you — can parse it.

Where it comes from

Stations are named with an ICAO code: 4 letters. In the mainland U.S. they start with K (KHOT, KOUN, KDFW). Alaska uses PA, Hawaii PH, Canada C, most of Europe E or L. The K is just a region prefix — KHOT is really airport "HOT."

2 · Anatomy, token by token

A METAR is just tokens separated by spaces, and they almost always come in the same order. Here's our example again, with each token labeled. Read it left to right:

KHOT · 041853Z · 18012G22KT · 10SM · FEW055 · SCT250 · 34/23 · A2996 · RMK AO2

Station & time

KHOT Station id — Hot Springs, AR.

041853Z Day-of-month 04, then time 1853, then Z. The Z means Zulu = UTC, the world's single shared clock. Weather ignores time zones on purpose so a report from Oklahoma and one from Germany can be compared directly. To get Central Time in summer, subtract 5 hours: 18:53 Z = 1:53 PM CDT. That Z / "Zulu" habit is worth burning in — every sounding, radar frame, and warning is stamped in UTC.

Wind

18012G22KT Three chunks glued together: direction 180° (where the wind comes from — 180 is due south), speed 012 = 12, and G22 = gusting to 22. KT = knots (1 knot ≈ 1.15 mph). No G? No gust reported. VRB05KT means the direction is variable (light and shifting). 00000KT is dead calm.

Direction is compass, not math

Wind direction is degrees on a compass: 360/0 = N, 090 = E, 180 = S, 270 = W. And it's always the direction the wind blows from. "180 at 12" is a south wind pushing north.

Visibility

10SM Visibility in Statute Miles. 10SM is the top value most U.S. stations report — basically "clear and far." 1/2SM is half a mile (fog or heavy rain). Lower = worse.

Sky & clouds

Cloud layers are a 3-letter cover code plus a 3-digit height in hundreds of feet. FEW055 = a few clouds at 5,500 ft. SCT250 = scattered at 25,000 ft. Add a zero-hundred: 055 → 5,500, 250 → 25,000. There can be several layers stacked lowest-to-highest. (Full code table in section 3.)

Temperature / dewpoint

34/23 Temperature 34 °C, dewpoint 23 °C, split by a slash, always in Celsius. A leading M means minus: M04/M08 is −4 °C over −8 °C. The gap between them is the dewpoint depression — small gap = humid, air near saturation; big gap = dry.

Dewpoint is the fuel gauge

A dewpoint in the low-to-mid 20s °C (70s °F) is juicy, unstable air — storm fuel. In the teens it's marginal; single digits or negative, storms starve. See the Field Manual for how dewpoint feeds instability. The decoder below flags juicy air for you.

Altimeter

A2996 A then four digits = the pressure setting in inches of mercury, decimal implied before the last two: A2996 = 29.96 inHg. Falling pressure often means weather moving in. (Outside the U.S. you'll see Q1013 instead — that's hectopascals, e.g. 1013 hPa.)

Remarks & present weather

Anything after RMK is remarks. AO2 just means an automated station that can tell rain from snow. Before RMK you may see present-weather codes — TS (thunderstorm), RA (rain), +RA (heavy rain), SN (snow), GR (hail). Those are the ones that make a chaser sit up. Full list next.

3 · The two code tables

Cloud cover

Reported by how many eighths ("oktas") of the sky a layer covers, with a height in hundreds of feet.

CodeMeansSky covered
SKC / CLRSky clear0/8 — nothing
FEWFew1/8 to 2/8
SCTScattered3/8 to 4/8
BKNBroken5/8 to 7/8
OVCOvercast8/8 — full lid
VVVertical visibilitysky hidden (fog); can't see up past this height

A CB or TCU tag after the height means that layer is a cumulonimbus (thunderstorm cloud) or towering cumulus (a storm trying to be born). BKN020CB = broken storm cloud with a base at 2,000 ft. That's a chaser's flag.

CAVOK

Sometimes a whole METAR just says CAVOK ("cav-oh-kay") for visibility and sky. It means Ceiling And Visibility OK: no clouds below 5,000 ft, no cumulonimbus, visibility 10 km or better, no significant weather. One word for "sky's great."

5 · Decode-it-yourself

No decoder for these — read them in your head first, then reveal the answer. This is the muscle you're building.

1. In 34/23, what's the dewpoint in °C? Is that juicy air?

Dewpoint = 23 °C (about 73 °F). The number after the slash is always the dewpoint. 23 °C is very juicy — that's storm fuel. Temp 34 / dew 23 is a hot, humid, primed afternoon.

2. Wind token 27032G45KT. Where's the wind from, and should you respect it?

From 270° = due west, sustained 32 knots (~37 mph), gusting 45 (~52 mph). Yes — respect it. That's strong enough to be a gust front or the back side of a squall line. Blowing dust, hard driving.

3. A METAR reads … 1/2SM TSRA BKN015CB …. What's happening at that airport?

Visibility down to half a mile in a thunderstorm with rain (TSRA), and a broken cumulonimbus base at 1,500 ft (BKN015CB). You're reading the core of an active storm — heavy rain cutting the visibility, storm cloud low overhead.

4. What does M04/M08 mean, and what's the dewpoint depression?

The M means minus: temperature −4 °C, dewpoint −8 °C. The dewpoint depression (temp minus dew) is 4 °C — a smallish gap, so the air is fairly moist and cold. Prime setup for snow or ice if precip falls.

5. You see 041853Z. It's summer and you're in Central Time. What's the local clock time?

The time is 18:53 UTC ("Zulu") on the 4th. Central Daylight Time is UTC−5, so 1:53 PM CDT. (In winter, CST is UTC−6, so it'd be 12:53 PM.) Always convert from Z — never assume the report is in your zone.
