SKEW-TThe one chart that tells you if storms will fire — and how bad

A radar shows you the storm that already exists. A Skew-T shows you the storm before it's born. It's a picture of the whole column of air stacked above one spot — how warm, how wet, and how windy at every height from the grass to the jet stream. Forecasters spend more time staring at this one chart than almost anything else, because the shape of it answers the only question that matters the night before a chase: is the atmosphere loaded, or is it a bust? It looks like a mess of slanted lines the first time you see it. It isn't. There are really only two curves you have to read. Let's learn to read them.

1 · Where the data comes from

Every chart starts with real measurements, and you can't measure the upper air from the ground. A thermometer on your porch tells you the temperature at your porch. It says nothing about the air two miles up, which is where storms actually build their engines. So twice a day the atmosphere gets measured from the inside — by a balloon.

The radiosonde

A radiosonde is a small foam box, about the size of a coffee cup, hanging under a hydrogen or helium weather balloon. As the balloon rises it stretches from a beachball to the size of a house, and the little box radios back its readings the whole way up:

  • Temperature — how warm the air is at each height.
  • Dewpoint (from a humidity sensor) — how much moisture is in it.
  • Pressure — which tells you the height it's currently at.
  • Wind — tracked by GPS, giving speed and direction at each level.

The balloon climbs for about two hours, past the cruising height of airliners, up to roughly 100,000 feet (~30 km), where the low pressure finally pops it. A tiny parachute drops the box back down. That single flight — one thin vertical line through the whole atmosphere — is called a sounding. Plot it on the right chart and you get a Skew-T.

Why twice a day, everywhere

It's launched at 00Z and 12Z — two fixed moments in Zulu (UTC) time, so the whole planet takes its snapshot at the same instant. About 800 sites worldwide do it, 92 of them in the United States (per the NWS). At exactly the same second, hundreds of balloons leave the ground from Alaska to Antarctica. Together those launches are the single biggest source of real, in-place data feeding every weather model on Earth. When a forecaster says a model run "looks good," it looks good because balloons like these agreed with it.

Cross-check the Gear Bench

The radiosonde is one of the instruments on your Gear Bench — the "measuring the air" section. Worth reading it there too, because this is a case where the professional instrument has no cheap substitute: you personally can't sample the air two miles up, so you rely on their balloon. Learning to read its output is how you borrow a $200 instrument for free, twice a day.

Why upper-air data beats a surface reading: a hot, sticky surface can sit under a lid of dry or warm air aloft that kills every storm — or under cold air aloft that lets storms explode. From the ground those two days feel identical. Only the sounding tells them apart. The surface is one number; the sounding is the whole story.

2 · Reading the axes — and what "skew" means

"Skew-T Log-P" is just a description of the two axes. Once you see them drawn, the scary name dissolves.

  • Up the left side = pressure, and pressure stands in for height. The ground is at the bottom, around 1000 mb. As you go up, pressure drops — 850, 700, 500, up to 200 mb near the jet stream at the top. (The "Log-P" part just means the pressure spacing is stretched so the important low levels near the ground get more room.)
  • Along the bottom = temperature, cold on the left, warm on the right — but the lines are tilted. That tilt is the "skew."

Why skew the temperature lines?

If temperature lines ran straight up-and-down, the temperature and dewpoint curves would crowd into a narrow vertical smear near the middle of the chart, impossible to read apart. So the constant-temperature lines are tilted 45° up and to the right. This spreads the whole picture across the page — it opens the curves out so you can actually see the gap between them, and it makes an important line (the path a rising parcel cools along) come out at a convenient angle. That's the entire trick. Skew = "we tilted the temperature grid so humans can read it."

3 · The two lines that matter

A real sounding draws two wiggly curves on that frame. Learn these two and you're 80% of the way there:

Temperature — how warm the air is at each height. Dewpoint — how much moisture is at each height.

The single most useful thing you can read is the gap between them:

  • Far apart = the air is dry at that height. Big spread near the ground often means it'll be hard for storms to survive.
  • Together (curves hugging) = the air is saturated — that layer is cloud, or about to be.

A real sounding: the red temperature curve always sits to the right of the green dewpoint curve. Near the ground they're close (moist air). Higher up they split apart (dry air). The gap is the humidity, read at a glance.

The LCL — where the cloud starts

Grab a bubble of surface air and shove it upward. As it rises it cools. Because it's cooling, at some height it gets cold enough that its moisture condenses — and that exact height is the LCL (Lifting Condensation Level). In plain terms: the LCL is the base of the cloud. It's why the flat bottoms of cumulus clouds all sit at the same height on a given day — every bubble of air hits its LCL at the same level. A low LCL (moist air, curves close together low down) means low cloud bases, which chasers like for tornadoes; a high LCL means the cloud base is way up and rain often evaporates before it reaches the ground.

4 · The parcel & CAPE — the storm's fuel

Now the payoff. Imagine picking up one "parcel" — a bubble — of warm surface air and letting it rise. Two things can happen:

  • The parcel cools as it climbs. If it's colder than the air around it, it's heavier than its surroundings, sinks back down, and nothing happens. Stable. Boring.
  • If the parcel stays warmer than the air around it, it's lighter — like a hot-air balloon — so it keeps rising on its own. That runaway rising is instability, and a rising column of warm moist air is a thunderstorm updraft.

On the chart, we draw the path the parcel would take (the dashed amber line) and compare it to the environment's real temperature curve (red). Where the parcel is warmer than the environment — the parcel line sits to the right of the red line — the parcel is buoyant. The area of that gap is the single most important number on the chart:

CAPE = Convective Available Potential Energy

CAPE is literally the size of that shaded area, measured in joules per kilogram (J/kg). It's the amount of fuel available to an updraft — how hard the air wants to rise. More area, more violent the storm can be. Rough field ranges:

  • ~1000 J/kg — marginal. Storms possible, usually not severe.
  • ~2500+ J/kg — strong instability. Serious severe-storm potential.
  • ~4000+ J/kg — extreme. Rare, explosive; the sky is a loaded spring.

The shaded red area is CAPE — the region where the rising parcel (amber) is warmer than the environment (red), so it accelerates upward on its own. That's the updraft's fuel. The small purple wedge low down is the cap.

The cap (CIN) — the lid on the pot

Look at that small purple wedge near 850 mb. There, the parcel is colder than the environment (a thin warm layer aloft — an inversion — sits on top of the cool surface). A rising bubble hits that warm layer and stalls, like a cork it can't push through. That's CIN — Convective Inhibition — usually just called the cap. It's negative energy: the parcel has to be forced through it before it can reach the free-rising CAPE above.

Why a broken cap means explosive storms

The cap sounds bad — it stops storms. But it's double-edged, and this is one of the most important ideas in severe weather. All morning, sunshine pumps heat and moisture into the air under the lid, and the CAPE above keeps building because nothing is releasing it. The pot is heating with the lid on. If afternoon heating or a front finally breaks the cap, all that stored CAPE releases at once through a few narrow holes — and you get sudden, violent, isolated supercells instead of a sky full of weak storms. A cap that holds too long = blue-sky bust. A cap that breaks at the right time = the biggest chase day of the year. Reading the cap is reading the day's whole personality.

5 · Two soundings, compared — shape into forecast

This is the whole skill in one move: you glance at the shape of a sounding and know what kind of day it is. Here are the two you'll meet most. Same chart frame, completely different days.

LOADED — supercell day

CAPPED / DRY — blue-sky bust

Read the shapes

LOADED (left): the temp and dewpoint curves hug each other down low (moist, sticky surface = high dewpoints), then a big fat CAPE area opens up above with no serious cap to stop it. The wind barbs on the right edge grow and turn with height — that's shear, which tilts an updraft and lets it rotate. Fat CAPE + strong shear + weak cap = the recipe for supercells and tornadoes. When a forecaster sees this shape, they start packing the car.

CAPPED / DRY (right): the dewpoint curve sits way to the left of the temperature curve — a huge spread, meaning dry air. There's a strong warm nose (the purple cap) that the parcel can't punch through, and barely any CAPE above it. This is a day that feels hot and stormy at the surface but produces nothing but a clear blue sky and disappointed chasers. Learning to spot this shape is how you avoid driving 400 miles for zero storms.

That's the entire payoff of the chart. You don't compute anything — you learn what "loaded" and "capped" look like, and after enough soundings your eye reads the day in about three seconds. Everything else is refinement.

6 · Why this is the summit

Be honest with yourself about where this sits: you don't need the Skew-T to start chasing safely. Radar and watching the sky come first. The Skew-T is the mountain you climb toward — it's the difference between reacting to storms that already exist and forecasting them the night before. It's a Rank 3 skill in the Academy: aspirational, and completely worth it. The professionals live on this chart precisely because it's the earliest, deepest look at what the atmosphere is about to do.

You can draw these yourself in code

Every sounding in this trainer was drawn by hand, but the real ones are generated by a Python library called MetPy — the same tool meteorologists use. It downloads a real balloon launch and plots the exact Skew-T you've been reading, computes the CAPE number, marks the LCL, everything. When you're ready, Code School is where you make your own. There is genuinely no better feeling than pulling this morning's real sounding from a station near you and reading the day off it — in a chart your own code drew.

Start by just looking. Pull up a real sounding (the SPC's site and the University of Wyoming both post them free from those twice-daily launches) and find the two curves. Ask only: are they close or far apart down low? Is there a fat CAPE area, and is there a cap on it? That's it. That's reading a Skew-T. The rest is a thousand soundings' worth of practice — and every serious forecaster started exactly where you are now, staring at the slanted lines until, one day, they clicked.
