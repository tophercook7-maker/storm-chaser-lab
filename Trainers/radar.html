RADAR TRAINERReading the Radar

A radar sweep is not a picture of the storm — it's a measurement. Two of them, actually. Learn what each color and shape is really telling you, learn the half-dozen signatures a spotter must recognize on sight, then prove it in the quiz at the bottom. Every diagram here is drawn by hand — no photos, works with the internet off.

Why this matters

Radar sees things your eyes can't: rotation buried in rain, a debris cloud at night, hail cores hiding in a green wall. But radar also lies by omission — the beam rises with distance and shoots over low-level features far from the site. You read radar to know where to point your eyes, never as a replacement for them. This trainer pairs with Field Manual §5–§8 (supercells, tornadoes, wind, hail).

1 · The Two Products

Every base radar image is one of two things. Learn to say which one you're looking at before anything else, because the same blob means totally different things in each.

Reflectivity — how much is falling

The radar sends out a pulse and measures how much energy bounces back. Bigger, denser, more numerous targets (raindrops, hailstones) bounce back more. That returned power is scaled in dBZ (decibels of reflectivity). Higher dBZ = heavier precipitation. It is the product you'll spend most of your time in — it shows you the shape of the storm.

20–35 dBZ · light rain 40–45 · moderate 50 · heavy 55–60 · very heavy / core 65+ · likely hail

The 60+ dBZ rule

Rain alone rarely tops ~55 dBZ. When you see a tight knot of 60 dBZ or higher — the deep red into pink/purple — suspect hail. Water reflects strongly, but hailstones are big, so a small area of very high dBZ punches above what any rain shaft can produce. High dBZ + a storm that's severe = hail core. (Confirm with dual-pol; see the hail signature below.)

Velocity — which way the wind blows

The same pulse comes back slightly shifted in frequency if the target is moving toward or away from the radar — that's the Doppler effect. The radar turns that shift into a wind picture, but only the part of the wind pointed along the beam (toward or away from the radar site). The convention is fixed and worth burning into memory:

RED = wind moving AWAY from the radar GREEN = wind moving TOWARD the radar gray = no motion / no data

The catch beginners miss

Velocity is relative to the radar, not to north. A storm can have strong winds and show almost no velocity color if those winds happen to blow across the beam instead of along it. And the beam climbs with range — 100+ miles out you're sampling the mid-levels, not the ground. Read velocity together with reflectivity and with your storm's distance from the site.

2 · The Signatures

These are the shapes and patterns that make a spotter's radar useful. For each: what it looks like, what it means, and — the part that matters — what you do about it.

Hook echo reflectivity · FM §5

The classic supercell fingerprint. The storm's rotating updraft (the mesocyclone) wraps precipitation around its back edge, curling it into a hook — usually on the storm's rear-right (southwest) flank for a storm moving northeast. The notch inside the hook is where the mesocyclone, and any tornado, tends to sit.

What you do

A hook says "check velocity now." A hook shape alone is suggestive, not proof — you confirm rotation in the velocity product (next signature). If a hook and a velocity couplet line up in the same spot, you treat that as a tornado threat, get positioned safely to the storm's southeast with an escape route, and — if you're a spotter — report it.

Velocity couplet & TVS velocity · FM §6

This is the real rotation signature. In the velocity product, find a spot where bright green sits right next to bright red, tightly packed. Green (toward the radar) touching red (away) over a small area means air is spinning: one side racing at you, the other racing away — a couplet. When it's very tight and strong it's flagged as a TVS (Tornadic Vortex Signature).

What you do

Line up the couplet with the hook on reflectivity. Same location, tight, and getting stronger sweep to sweep? That's a rotating updraft that can produce or is producing a tornado. Position southeast of it with an escape route, keep it in sight, and report. Tighter and lower (a couplet close to the radar, so the beam is near the ground) is more dangerous than a loose one far away where the beam is high aloft.

Tornado Debris Signature (TDS) & dual-pol 3 products · FM §6

Modern radars are dual-polarization: they send pulses both horizontally and vertically, which lets them tell the shape of what they're hitting. Rain is uniform, so it comes back "clean" — a high correlation coefficient (CC), near 1.0. Tornado debris — boards, dirt, roofing, shredded trees — is a chaotic mix of shapes and sizes, so CC drops. A "debris ball" is a knot of high reflectivity at the tornado's spot, colocated with a couplet in velocity and a CC hole. All three together = a tornado is on the ground, lofting debris, right now.

What you do

A confirmed TDS means the tornado is real and lofting debris — this is not a "maybe." If it's near you, you should already be in shelter, not watching. As a spotter far enough to be safe, this is the highest- confidence report you can make. It also means people downstream are in danger now. Never drive toward a TDS to "see it." The lab keeps the name El Reno 2013 for a reason: an experienced chase team was killed when a violent tornado they were tracking turned and widened faster than they could escape. Radar told the story; the road didn't cooperate.

Bounded Weak Echo Region (BWER) reflectivity · FM §5

A BWER (or "vault") is a hole of weak reflectivity wrapped by strong echo, seen on a vertical slice or implied by the storm's structure. It exists because the updraft is so strong that raindrops and small hail get carried upward faster than they can grow and fall — so that column reads weak even though violent things are happening in it. A BWER = a powerful, well-organized updraft: the engine of a supercell.

What you do

A BWER doesn't tell you a tornado is on the ground — it tells you this storm is strong and organized and deserves your full attention. Treat it as a supercell: expect large hail near/under the vault, and watch the velocity data closely for rotation developing on its flank.

Bow echo reflectivity · FM §7

A line of storms that bulges outward in the middle like an archer's bow. The bulge is a burst of strong straight-line winds shoving the line forward — the storm is producing damaging downburst winds, not rotation. A long-lived, widespread windstorm made of bowing lines is a derecho. Look also for a small notch behind the apex (a rear-inflow notch) feeding dry, fast air into the push.

What you do

Expect a wall of wind and driving rain at and just ahead of the apex — treat it like a severe wind event, not a tornado hunt. Get indoors or well clear; do not park downwind of the apex. Trees down, power out, and blown-out visibility are the hazards. Report measured or estimated wind gusts and damage.

Three-body scatter spike (TBSS) reflectivity · FM §8

A give-away for large hail. Very big hailstones scatter the beam so hard that some energy takes a detour — hail → ground → hail → radar — and arrives late. The radar plots that late energy as a faint spike of echo pointing straight away from the radar, directly behind a strong core. There's nothing actually there; it's an artifact. But the artifact only happens with hail big enough to make it, so a TBSS (also called a "hail spike" or "flying eagle") is a strong hail flag.

What you do

Treat a TBSS as confirmation of significant hail in that core. Stay out of the core and its forward path — hail can smash windshields and, driven by wind, injure. If you're spotting, a TBSS plus a 60+ dBZ knot is a solid large-hail report.

Reading order that pros use

  1. Reflectivity first — find the storm's shape. Hook? Bow? Tight 60+ core?
  2. Velocity next — is there a couplet under that hook? Green beside red, tight and low?
  3. Dual-pol to confirm — CC hole for debris, high dBZ + big drops for hail.
  4. Then look outside. Radar aims your eyes; your eyes make the call. And you never do any of it alone — see the note below.

Radar is a map, not a shield

Everything on this page helps you understand a storm — it does not make it safe to be near one. Actual chasing is done years from now, never alone, with a trained adult, in a vehicle with a real escape route, and only after a lot of practice reading exactly these signatures. The lab keeps El Reno, 2013 — where a violent tornado killed experienced chasers who couldn't outrun it — as the standing reminder that the radar can be perfectly clear and the situation still be deadly. Learn the screen now. Earn the field later.
