THE ACADEMYThe path — how you level up

Everything else in this lab is a room full of tools. This page is the map that tells you which room to walk into next. You start knowing nothing and you finish as a trained spotter who can look at a screen and a sky and say what's happening — and what's about to. Follow the four ranks in order. Check things off as you go: the boxes are saved on the drive, so the whole lab remembers where you are, even after you close it and come back next month.

Why ranks instead of a to-do list

A flat list of 40 things is paralyzing — you don't know what matters or what depends on what. Ranks fix that. Each rank is a real level of skill, and each one unlocks the next because it actually gives you what the next one needs. You don't move up because you finished chores. You move up because you can do the thing the rank is named for. That's how real spotters are trained: reading first, radar second, data third, the road last — years last.

One rule before you start

Nothing on this whole page asks you to go stand near a storm. Rank 1 through 3 happen at a desk, at a window, and in your yard. Rank 4 is about understanding what real chasing takes and why it's years away — done with training and an experienced adult, and never alone. Keep that straight the whole way up and this path is completely safe.

1
Rank One

Rookie

Who you are here: you watch the sky and you know what you're looking at. You can name the cloud, name the storm, and tell whether it's the boring kind or the kind that's worth watching.

When you finish: you can walk outside, look up, and put a name on what's over your head — and open a live radar without it being a wall of noise.

Do these in order

Checkpoint · answer before you level up

1) Name the four storm types.   2) You see a tall, hard, cauliflower cloud with a flat anvil spreading off the top — what is it called, and what does seeing one tell you?   3) What's the difference between watching the sky and just being outside?

2
Rank Two

Spotter

Who you are here: you can read radar and you're SKYWARN-trained. You're not guessing from the picture anymore — you know what the colors and the two radar modes are actually telling you.

When you finish: you can open live radar, find the dangerous storm on it, and know the difference between what radar shows and what it's hiding.

Do these in order

Checkpoint · answer before you level up

1) What is a hook echo, and which radar mode shows rotation directly?   2) What's the difference between a watch and a warning?   3) Why do spotters trust dewpoint over relative humidity?   4) Name one thing radar physically cannot see.

3
Rank Three

Analyst

Who you are here: you can look at the data before the storm exists and say what the sky is going to do. You've crossed from reading what's happening to forecasting what's coming.

When you finish: you can read the morning's numbers and a sounding, write your own little nowcast, and you're feeding real data into a national science project.

Do these in order

Checkpoint · answer before you level up

1) What do CAPE and shear together tell you — and why is one without the other not enough?   2) On a simple sounding, where is the CAPE and what does a fat CAPE area mean?   3) What does your nowcast program do, in one sentence?

4
Rank Four

Chaser-in-training

Who you are here: you know what chasing actually takes — the science degree, the years, the danger, the money — and you're building toward it the honest way instead of the movie way.

When you finish: you understand the whole real road, you've started the two things that actually open it (advanced math and the science path), and you know why the car part comes last.

This rank is about the truth, not the road

Nothing here puts you in a car. Real chasing is done years from now, with training and an experienced adult, and never alone. The lab is blunt about why: on May 31, 2013, near El Reno, Oklahoma, three of the most experienced storm researchers alive — Tim Samaras, his son Paul Samaras, and Carl Young — were killed when the tornado turned. If it can take them, the lesson is permanent: knowledge first, respect always, and you do not do this alone or before you're trained.

Do these in order

Checkpoint · answer before you call yourself trained-in-progress

1) What are the core math and science requirements to become a meteorologist (the degree, the classes)?   2) What is the one rule about chasing that never bends?   3) Why does the car come last?

Past this rank

There isn't a Rank 5 on this drive, because the next levels don't happen on a flash drive — they happen in a classroom, in a college, and years from now in the field. What you've built here is the thing all of that stands on. Keep the Chase Log going. Keep the math up. Keep looking up.

How to use this page

Open the Academy first, every time. Find the highest rank where you still have unchecked boxes, and do the next one. That's it — you never have to guess what to do next. The checkmarks live on the drive, so you can walk away for a week or a month and pick up exactly where you left off. Don't skip ahead to Rank 4 because it's the exciting one; every rank is built on the one before it, and skipping just means the fun stuff won't make sense yet.

The most important instrument

It isn't the radar, the Pi, or any radio. It's a trained brain plus the habit of writing down what you see. A spotter who logs every sky beats a spotter with expensive gear and no memory. So the real graduation from this Academy isn't a certificate — it's the day keeping your Chase Log feels automatic. Start today. Add one line tonight.
