THE FIELD MANUAL How storms work, and how to read them.

This is the reference. You don't read it front to back in one sitting — you come back to it when the Deck shows you something you don't recognize. Every fact in here was checked against a National Weather Service, Storm Prediction Center, or NSSL source. Where the science is uncertain, it says so. That's not a weakness of this manual. That's the actual state of the field.

Official published NWS/SPC/NSSL definition Rule of thumb forecaster convention, no official source Research from a specific study — describes a sample, not a law

Why the labels matter

Half of what gets repeated about severe weather online is a rule of thumb wearing a lab coat. Somebody says "60 dBZ means large hail" like it's a law of physics. It isn't — NWS Norman's own spotter glossary says "there are no reliable threshold values to confirm the presence of hail or severe weather in a given situation." Learning to tell an official definition from a useful habit is most of what separates someone who sounds like they know storms from someone who does.

1 · How a thunderstorm is built

Every thunderstorm on Earth needs the same three things. Severe storms need a fourth.

IngredientWhat it doesWhere you see it
Moisture Fuel. Water vapor releases heat when it condenses, and that heat is what drives the updraft. Dewpoint. Not humidity — dewpoint.
Instability Air near the ground is warmer/lighter than the air above it, so once it starts rising it keeps going. CAPE.
Lift Something to give it the first shove — a cold front, a dryline, a sea breeze, a mountain, an outflow boundary from an earlier storm. Fronts and boundaries on the map.
Wind shear
(severe only)		 Wind changing speed and/or direction with height. This is what turns a storm from a blob into an organized machine that can last for hours and rotate. 0–6 km bulk shear. SRH.

Dewpoint is the number to watch

Relative humidity tells you how close the air is to saturated at its current temperature — which means it moves every time the temperature moves, and it tells you almost nothing about how much water is actually in the air. Dewpoint tells you the actual moisture content. It's the number in the Storm Deck's conditions panel, and it's why Mission 4 has you write a function that keys on it.

Careful here — this is where people confidently state a fake number

You will hear "you need a 65° dewpoint for tornadoes." The SPC publishes no dewpoint threshold at all. Neither does any NWS severe criteria document — SPC's outlook definitions contain no moisture criterion whatsoever. Higher dewpoints generally mean more fuel, and that's real. But the specific number is Rule of thumb, and a 60° dewpoint in a cool airmass with steep lapse rates can absolutely produce a tornado.

The cap

Sometimes there's a layer of warm air aloft acting like a lid. Rising parcels hit it and stop. The formal names are lid and cap. The energy it takes to punch through is CIN — convective inhibition, the "negative area" a parcel has to overcome before it can free-fall upward. Official

A cap isn't purely bad. A weak cap lets everything go up at once and you get a mess of competing junk storms. A strong cap holds the energy back until something forces one storm through — and that one storm gets all of it. That's how you end up with a single monster in an otherwise clear sky.

"Loaded gun" — say it right

You'll hear that phrase for a capped, high-CAPE atmosphere. The NWS glossary does carry it — tagged as slang. The formal terms are lid and cap. Use those when you're writing a report. Save the slang for talking to your friends.

2 · The four storm types

Shear is the thing that decides which of these you get. Little shear → the first one. Lots of shear → the last one.

Single cell ("pulse")

One updraft, one downdraft. Life span 20–30 minutes. It kills itself: the rain-cooled downdraft falls right back through its own updraft and cuts off the inflow. Can still drop marginally severe hail or a wet microburst on the way down. Mostly a summer-afternoon storm.

Multicell cluster

A group of cells in different stages of life, feeding each other. As one dies, its cold outflow lifts warm air along the leading edge and builds the next one. Lasts for hours because it's constantly replacing itself. Most severe hail and wind in the country comes from these.

Squall line / bow echo

Cells organized into a line, often hundreds of miles long. The whole line has a shared cold pool of outflow air out front. When a segment of the line surges forward because of an intense rear inflow jet, it bends into a bow echo — and that's your damaging straight-line wind signature. Bow echoes can also spin up brief tornadoes at the ends and along the leading edge.

Straight-line wind from a bow echo can hit tornado-strength speeds. Wind damage is not proof of a tornado. Damage direction is: a tornado throws debris in converging directions; straight-line wind lays everything down the same way.

Supercell

A thunderstorm with a persistently rotating updraft. That's the entire definition — rotation is what makes it a supercell, not size or violence. That rotating updraft is called a mesocyclone, and it's the reason a supercell can last for many hours and produce the biggest hail and nearly all strong tornadoes.

Supercells are commonly associated with 0–6 km vertical shear of 35–40 knots and greater. Official (SPC)

3 · Supercell anatomy

Learn these parts by name. When you give a report, the name is the report.

PartWhat it is
UpdraftThe rising column. In a supercell it's rotating. Visually it's the hard, crisp, cauliflower tower — not the mushy stuff.
MesocycloneOfficial "A storm-scale region of rotation, typically around 2–6 miles in diameter." It is a radar term. See the warning below.
FFD
forward flank downdraft		The main precipitation core — the heavy rain and hail, ahead of and to the left of the updraft.
RFD
rear flank downdraft		Official "A region of dry air subsiding on the back side of, and wrapping around, a mesocyclone. It often is visible as a clear slot wrapping around the wall cloud." The RFD is what carves the hook you see on radar — and it's deeply involved in tornado formation.

AnvilThe flat top where the updraft hits the stratosphere and spreads out. A hard, sharply-defined anvil edge with an overshooting top punching above it means a violently strong updraft.

The mistake almost everybody makes

From the NWS Glossary, verbatim: "Properly used, mesocyclone is a radar term… Therefore, a mesocyclone should not be considered a visually-observable phenomenon."

You cannot see a mesocyclone. You can see a rotating wall cloud, you can see curved inflow bands that imply one — but if you report "I see a mesocyclone," a trained meteorologist knows immediately that you don't know the vocabulary. Report the wall cloud. Report the rotation. Let the radar name the mesocyclone.

The three supercell flavors

  • Classic — the textbook one. You can see the updraft base and the wall cloud, with the precipitation core off to the side. This is the one in every photo.
  • HP (high precipitation) — rain wraps all the way around the mesocyclone. The tornado can be completely hidden inside the rain. These are the ones that kill spotters and chasers, and they're the common flavor in the South and the Mid-South, including Arkansas.
  • LP (low precipitation) — very little rain, gorgeous corkscrew structure, you can see everything. Mostly a High Plains storm. Big hail, tornadoes rare.

4 · Wall cloud vs shelf cloud

This is the single most important thing your eyes will ever do, and it is the identification new spotters get wrong most often. Both are a dark, dramatic lowering of the cloud. They mean completely opposite things.

Wall cloudShelf cloud
Where on the storm Under the rain-free base, on the back/inflow side On the leading edge, out front
Attached to The updraft The outflow — cold air pushing out
The wind on your face Blowing toward the storm (you're in the inflow) Blowing from the storm, and it hits you hard and cold
Motion Air rising into it. May rotate. Air rising along the front edge, sinking behind. Looks like a wedge or plow.
Means Possible tornado. Report it. Gust front. Strong straight-line wind coming. Not a tornado.

The test that actually works

Feel the wind. If wind is blowing into the storm behind that lowering — toward it, feeding it — you are looking at a wall cloud. If cold wind is blasting out at you from under it, that's a shelf cloud and the gust front is about to arrive. Photographs lie about this constantly. Standing there with wind on your skin does not.

Scud, and why you should not report it

Ragged, fast-moving fragments of cloud below the base. Scud forms and dissipates in seconds, often moves in a completely different direction from the storm, and is not attached to the cloud base in a solid way. It looks alarming in photographs. It has fooled a lot of people into calling in funnel clouds.

A funnel is smooth, persistent, and rotating. Watch it for thirty seconds before you call. If it's shredding, reforming, or drifting sideways with no rotation, it's scud.

5 · Reading reflectivity

Reflectivity is the "how much stuff is up there" picture. It's the colorful one everybody knows. The unit is dBZ, and the scale runs from about −35 dBZ to +85 dBZ. Official

Why it's a weird logarithmic unit — and the trap inside it

Raw reflectivity varies insanely: a tiny drizzle droplet returns about 0.001 mm⁶/m³ while hail in a severe storm returns around 36,000,000. Nobody can plot that. So the radar takes the logarithm and you get decibels.

Here's the part that will change how you read radar. Power returned to the radar goes as the target's diameter to the sixth power. NOAA's own worked example:

Two boxes, same color, very different weather

Box A: 729 raindrops, each 1 mm across. Box B: one raindrop, 3 mm across.

Both boxes read 29 dBZ. Identical color on the map. But the rainfall rate in Box A is 22 times heavier. A few big drops can look exactly like a lot of small ones. This is why reflectivity alone can never tell you what's actually falling — and it's the whole reason dual-pol exists.

General reflectivity guidelines Official

NOAA publishes this table and frames it explicitly as a guideline: "Generally speaking, the higher the reflectivity, the heavier the precipitation. However, this is not always the case."

dBZExpected intensity
> 65Extremely heavy precipitation including water-coated hail
50 – 65Heavy precipitation or some hail
40 – 50Moderate precipitation
20 – 40Light precipitation
0 – 20Very light precipitation, or general clutter
−35 – 0Extremely light — drizzle, snow

What you may not say

"50 dBZ or more normally are associated with heavy thunderstorms, perhaps with hail" is real — that's NWS Norman. But the same sentence continues: "as with most other quantities, there are no reliable threshold values to confirm the presence of hail or severe weather in a given situation."

The widely-repeated "60 dBZ = large hail" is not current NWS guidance and does not appear on any current NOAA page. Don't repeat it. Modern hail identification on NWS products is done by the dual-pol classifier, not by raw dBZ.

6 · Reading velocity

Reflectivity shows you where the stuff is. Velocity shows you where it's going — and velocity is where tornadoes live. This is the harder product and it's the one that separates people who look at radar from people who read it.

GREENmoving TOWARD the radar
(negative values)
REDmoving AWAY from the radar
(positive values)

Official — and it comes from the earliest days of Doppler radar, when the radars pointed straight up. Note that on many displays the very strongest inbound winds render blue, not green.

The limitation that governs everything

Doppler radar measures only the component of motion along the beam — toward it or away from it. A wind blowing perfectly sideways across the beam shows up as zero. Not "weak." Zero. That's why the radar you choose matters, why a feature can look strong from one radar and invisible from another, and why the same storm reads differently from two sites. Everything in the velocity product is radial velocity.

The velocity couplet — the pattern that matters

Rotation shows up as bright green right next to bright red. Air on one side of the vortex is coming at the radar; air on the other side is going away. Two intense, adjacent, opposite colors in a small area is a couplet, and a tight one at low levels is what a warning meteorologist is looking for.

The key word is tight. A big, loose green/red gradient across half the storm is just the storm-scale wind field. What you want is strong, opposite, and close together — the tighter the couplet, the more concentrated the rotation.

Aliasing — when the radar reports the wrong sign

Radar figures out speed from the phase shift of the returned pulse. If the phase shift goes past 180°, the radar can't tell which way it went. The maximum speed it can measure unambiguously is the Nyquist velocity. Past that, the value folds — and it folds to the opposite end of the scale.

NSSL's numeric example: with a Nyquist velocity of ±30 m/s, a true +36 m/s is displayed as −24 m/s. Strong outbound is shown as strong inbound. And in an extreme case, a true ±60 m/s can fold all the way to zero. Official

How you spot it

Aliasing produces "an abrupt change in color from one end of the velocity cointerval to the other" — a hard, unnatural jump with no gradient between. Real rotation has structure around it; an alias just slams from one extreme to the other. NSSL is blunt that a single aliased pixel is undetectable on its own — only the surrounding pattern gives it away.

Range folding — the purple that isn't what you think

When the radar can't determine whether a return is inbound or outbound, it flags it RF (range folded) and — on most displays — paints it purple. NWS calls it "purple haze." Within those areas the velocity could not be resolved and none is displayed. Official

The trap that will get you exactly once

Purple on the VELOCITY product = range folding, no data.
Purple on the REFLECTIVITY product = very high dBZ. The opposite of "no data" — it's the most intense return on the map.

Same color, two products, opposite meanings. NWS training says it outright: "the color purple on Reflectivity products indicates very high dBZ values, not range folding." Also know that purple isn't universal — NOAA's own JetStream shows range folding in white. It's a color-table convention, not a standard. The letters RF are the reliable tell.

The Doppler Dilemma

Here's a genuinely elegant piece of physics. The radar's maximum range and its maximum measurable velocity are inversely related — you cannot have both. NSSL writes it as Vmax · rmax = ±cλ/8: a constant. Push one up, the other goes down.

ProductPulse rate (PRF)Max rangeMax velocity
ReflectivityLow, ~320/sec290 miles18 mph
VelocityHigh, ~1300/sec72 miles71 mph

Official This is why the WSR-88D scans the lowest elevation angles twice — once slow for range, once fast for velocity. It's also why velocity data gets unreliable far from the radar while reflectivity keeps going. The radar you pick in my-settings.js is not a cosmetic choice.

7 · The signatures

Hook echo

Official NWS Glossary: "A radar reflectivity pattern characterized by a hook-shaped extension of a thunderstorm echo, usually in the right-rear part of the storm (relative to its direction of motion). A hook often is associated with a mesocyclone, and indicates favorable conditions for tornado development."

NSSL on the mechanism: "The hook is caused by the rear flank downdraft and is the result of precipitation wrapping around the back side of the updraft." So a hook is really a picture of the RFD doing its work. It's a fairly shallow feature — typically only 3–4 km deep.

Not "the southwest side." Right-rear relative to storm motion.

You'll read "southwest flank" a lot, and for a typical storm moving southwest-to-northeast it happens to be true. But it's Rule of thumb that quietly assumes a storm direction. A storm moving northwest-to-southeast will put its hook somewhere else entirely, and a spotter who memorized "southwest" will be staring at the wrong quadrant. Learn it as right-rear relative to motion and you're never wrong.

A weaker, less-defined version has its own official name: pendant echo. Operational NWS training also describes the classic hook as "shaped like a figure six."

Historical note worth knowing: the first time a hook echo was directly identified with a tornado was April 9, 1953, on a research radar at Willard Airport in Savoy, Illinois, watching a tornado near Champaign. That's confirmed by NWS Lincoln, IL. The entire signature you're learning is barely seventy years old.

Mesocyclone

A rotation signature on Doppler velocity meeting specific criteria for magnitude, vertical depth, and duration. Typically 2–6 miles in diameter. Appears as a solid circle on NWS velocity products.

TVS — Tornadic Vortex Signature

Official NWS Glossary: "Doppler radar signature in the radial velocity field indicating intense, concentrated rotation — more so than a mesocyclone." Like a mesocyclone it has to meet criteria for strength, depth, and time continuity. A TVS substantially raises the likelihood of a tornado. It does not guarantee one, and — like a mesocyclone — it cannot be seen with your eyes.

NSSL researchers discovered the TVS, and one of the useful things about it is that it often appears on radar several kilometers above the ground before a tornado reaches the ground. That's lead time.

Bow echo

A line segment that bulges forward, driven by a rear inflow jet. Your damaging straight-line wind signature. The ends of the bow (the "book end vortices") and the leading edge can also produce brief spin-up tornadoes.

8 · Dual-pol, and seeing debris in the air

In 2012 the entire WSR-88D network was upgraded to dual polarization. Before that the radar sent out a purely horizontal pulse. Now it sends horizontal and vertical, and by comparing the two returns it can tell you about the size and shape of what it's hitting. This is the biggest single leap in severe weather detection in your lifetime.

ProductWhat it measuresHow to read it
ZDR
differential reflectivity		 Horizontal power minus vertical power. Effectively: is the target wide or tall? >0 = flattened → big raindrops (they flatten as they fall).
~0 = round or tumbling → hail, sleet, small drops.
<0 = tall/thin → ice crystals, elongated snowflakes.
CC
correlation coefficient		 How similarly the horizontal and vertical pulses behaved. Unitless, 0 to 1. >0.97 = uniform met targets — all rain, all snow.
0.80–0.97 = mixed — hail, rain/snow mix, sleet.
<0.80 = not weather — birds, bugs, buildings, smoke… or debris.
KDP
specific differential phase		 Phase shift, not power — how much the beam is slowed by what it passes through. Excellent for heavy rainfall rate. Mostly immune to partial beam blockage, because it doesn't depend on returned power at all.

The Wylie, Texas case — why ZDR is beautiful

A supercell showed reflectivity over 65 dBZ with ZDR near 0 dB. Enormous return, but perfectly round-looking targets. Round means tumbling, and what tumbles is hail. That storm produced 5-inch hail. Reflectivity alone said "something huge." ZDR said "and it's ice."

TDS — the Tornado Debris Signature

This is the one that will stop you cold the first time you see it. A tornado on the ground lofts insulation, lumber, dirt, roofing. That debris is not weather — it's random junk, tumbling in every orientation. So the radar's correlation coefficient collapses in exactly the spot where the couplet is.

The original research definition (Ryzhkov et al., 2005) lists five criteria:

  1. a hook echo
  2. CC < 0.8
  3. a pronounced vortex signature in the velocity field
  4. ZDR < 0.5 dB
  5. Z > 45 dBZ

Research The authors add that criterion 2 — the CC drop — "probably has the best discriminating power."

Two numbers, both correct

You'll see CC < 0.8 and you'll also see up to 0.95. Both are real. 0.8 is the original research threshold; NWS operational training uses the looser value provided it's a local CC minimum collocated with a strong vortex. The reason is physical: rain gets entrained into the debris cloud and pulls CC back up. When two sources disagree like this, the right move isn't to pick one — it's to understand why they differ.

A TDS means a tornado is on the ground right now and it is doing damage. It is not a forecast. It's a confirmation. If you ever see one over a town, that is one of the most sobering images in this entire field.

9 · What radar can't see

This section is the one that makes you actually good. Anyone can learn what a hook echo looks like. Knowing when the radar is lying to you by omission is the skill.

The beam goes up, and it gets fat

The radar beam leaves the dish at a slight upward angle, and the Earth curves away underneath it. So the farther out you look, the higher above the ground you're looking. It also spreads: about 1,000 feet wider for every 10 miles of travel. At 120 miles out, the beam is over 2 miles wide. Official

Jarrell, Texas — the same tornado from two radars

Central TX radar, 20 miles awayFort Worth radar, 98 miles away
Beam height above ground~1,300 ft~11,600 ft
Beam width~2,000 ft~2 miles
What you can seeLow-level structureNWS: "the radar is no longer scanning the important low level features"

Same storm. Same moment. One radar sees the tornado's structure; the other is looking two miles over the top of it and smearing everything into a blob. This is why the radar station you set in my-settings.js matters more than any other setting in the lab. A distant radar cannot see low-level rotation. Not "sees it poorly." Cannot see it.

The algorithms are worse than you think — and you should know the numbers

The radar has automated detection algorithms. People assume they're near-perfect. They are not, and NOAA has published exactly how imperfect. From Mitchell et al. (1998), Weather and Forecasting, tested on an independent dataset of 31 tornadoes:

AlgorithmPOD
how often it catches a real tornado		FAR
how often it cries wolf
NSSL Tornado Detection Algorithm43%48%

Research

Read that bottom row again — it's a lesson in how statistics lie

A 0% false alarm rate looks perfect. It's the opposite. That algorithm scored 0% FAR because it almost never fires at all — 5 hits against 165 misses. The paper says so plainly: it "produces very few false alarms but is compromised by an extremely low probability of detection." Any time someone shows you a metric that looks flawless, ask what the other metric was doing.

And for the mesocyclone detection algorithms, the false alarm ratio for tornadoes runs 67% to 97%. Which means:

A mesocyclone detection is a weak tornado predictor on its own

Most rotating storms never produce a tornado. This is exactly why the National Weather Service still needs you. The radar can tell them a storm is rotating. Only a person on the ground can tell them what actually happened.

It's worse where you live

The same 1998 study broke performance out by region:

WhereTornado detection POD
Plains-type events68%
Non-plains events25%

Arkansas is not the Plains. Storms here are more often rain-wrapped HP supercells and squall-line spin-ups, at night, in hilly, tree-covered terrain, where the tornado is invisible and the radar algorithms perform at roughly a third of their Plains skill.

The authors note it's a small sample and more data is needed — that caveat is theirs and it's fair. But the direction of the effect is real and it means something specific for you: the place where radar is weakest is the place where a trained spotter is worth the most. That's not a consolation prize. That's the job description.

The memory device everybody uses: a watch means the ingredients are on the counter. A warning means it's in the oven.

The severe criteria — memorize these three

A thunderstorm is officially severe if it produces any one of:

  • Hail 1.00 inch (quarter size) or larger
  • Wind 58 mph or greater
  • A tornado

Note what's not on that list: lightning, heavy rain, and how scary it looks. A storm can be terrifying and not be severe. A storm can be severe and look ordinary. The criteria are the criteria.

11 · The numbers a forecaster actually looks at

You will grow into this section. Don't try to memorize it today — come back when you start reading SPC mesoscale discussions and want to know what the letters mean.

CAPE — Convective Available Potential Energy

Official "The integrated 'positive area' in J/kg (from the LFC to EL) for the lifted parcel." In plain terms: how much fuel is loaded, and therefore how strong the updraft can be.

CAPE (J/kg)SPC forecasters call it
< 1000weak instability
1000 – 2500moderate instability
2500 – 4000strong instability
> 4000extreme instability

An honest note about that table

SPC publishes it — but the exact framing is "SPC forecasters often refer to." That's describing in-house vocabulary on an explanatory help page, not a defined operational criterion. It appears in no NWS directive. The AMS Glossary defines CAPE mathematically and endorses no qualitative categories at all. Different NWS offices publish slightly different breakpoints with different words. Use it, cite it as an SPC convention, and don't treat it as a law.

You'll also see three flavors depending on which parcel of air you lift: SBCAPE (from the surface), MLCAPE (mean of the lowest 100 mb), and MUCAPE (the most unstable parcel in the lowest 300 mb). They can differ enormously — one study found median SBCAPE of 1492 J/kg against median MLCAPE of 685 for the same soundings. Which one somebody quotes changes the story. Always ask which parcel.

LCL height — the most underrated number here

The lifting condensation level is where a rising parcel saturates. It's essentially cloud base height. Low cloud base means a moist, humid boundary layer — and it turns out to matter a lot for tornadoes.

From Thompson et al. (2003), 413 proximity soundings Research:

And the single cleanest sentence in that whole paper: "MLLCL heights did not exceed 1750 m for any of our 54 significantly tornadic supercell soundings."

Not one. In fifty-four cases. That's the kind of statement worth remembering — not because it's a rule (it's a sample, and samples have edges), but because it tells you which direction to look.

A habit that will make you unusually good at this

When you read a number about the atmosphere, ask two questions: who published it, and is it a definition, a study, or a habit? Most people never ask either one. You now have a manual that labels every number in it. Keep doing that on your own and in five years you'll be able to tell, in about ten seconds, whether someone talking about weather actually knows anything.

12 · Safety

The rules, in order

  1. You spot from a safe, fixed location. Home. Not the car, not "just up the road."
  2. You never go outside to look at a tornado-warned storm. If a warning is out for your location, you are in your safe place — lowest floor, interior room, away from windows.
  3. You do not chase. Not alone, not with friends, not to the edge of town.
  4. Not in a car. See below — the Storm Prediction Center is unusually blunt about this.
  5. Not under an overpass. This one has its own section, because it has killed people and the reason usually given for it is wrong.
  6. At night, you see nothing. Southern tornadoes are disproportionately nocturnal and rain-wrapped. Your eyes are useless. That's what NOAA Weather Radio is for — a tone-alert radio with battery backup is the single best piece of severe weather equipment a family can own. In the Little Rock area it's 162.550 MHz.

Whose rule is rule 3?

Honesty about sourcing, since this manual keeps insisting on it: the National Weather Service has no national minimum age for spotters, and no national policy saying minors can't chase. Individual offices set their own — NWS Green Bay requires 15 or older; most publish nothing. So rule 3 is this manual's rule, not a federal one, and it is: you don't chase until you can drive yourself out of trouble. That's the actual line. It isn't about age, it's about whether you control the escape route.

The overpass — the myth that keeps killing people

In 1991 a news crew in Kansas sheltered under a highway overpass, filmed a tornado passing, and survived. That video has been on television ever since, and it taught a generation of Americans something false.

On May 3, 1999, during the Oklahoma tornado outbreak, tornadoes crossed interstate highways at seven locations. At three of them, an overpass was directly in the damage path. All three produced a fatality. Research

The third one is the one to remember

That was an F2. Not a monster. The kind of tornado people casually assume is survivable. The researchers' own conclusion: a tornado need not be large or violent to kill you at an overpass.

WHY — AND WHY THE USUAL EXPLANATION IS WRONG

You will hear that an overpass acts like a Venturi tube and speeds the wind up. Skip that one. It's not the National Weather Service's explanation, and a 2021 wind-tunnel and computational study found no meaningful flow acceleration under realistic overpass shapes. It's a contested mechanism dressed up as a fact — exactly the thing the labels at the top of this manual exist to catch.

Here are the reasons that actually hold up, and you can reason your way to the first two yourself:

  1. Wind gets faster the higher you go. Friction with the ground slows the air nearest it. So when you climb up into the girders to get away from the tornado, you are climbing into faster wind. In the NWS's words: "By climbing up underneath the overpass, people are moving into a place where the wind speeds typically will be higher." You're not hiding from it. You're climbing toward it.
  2. It isn't the wind that kills you — it's what's in the wind. An overpass is a wide-open horizontal tube pointed straight into the debris stream. Every board, every sheet of roofing, every fistful of gravel in that tornado gets funneled through the exact space you're standing in, with nothing in between.
  3. There's usually nothing to hold onto. The famous 1991 bridge had an unusual crawl space and an exposed beam. Most don't — the Payne County overpass was smooth concrete. You cannot count on finding a bridge shaped like the one on TV.
  4. The wind reverses 180°. As the tornado passes, the flow flips direction. Whatever you braced against becomes the wrong side, in the middle of it.
  5. Bridges aren't rated for it. They're engineered to hold weight pressing down, not wind pushing sideways for a sustained period. An overpass is not a shelter; it's a road.
  6. You trap everyone behind you. Cars stopping under a bridge dam the interstate, stranding drivers who could otherwise have driven clear. The NWS puts this one in capitals: "WE MUST AVOID FORCING OTHERS INTO THE SAME DESPERATE SITUATION BY BLOCKING THE ROAD!"

Reason 6 is the grown-up one

The first five are about what happens to you. The sixth is about what your decision does to strangers. Most safety rules are personal-risk arguments; this one isn't. If you ever have to explain to an adult why you won't stop under a bridge, that's the argument that tends to land.

If you're in a vehicle — the honest version

The Storm Prediction Center does not soften this, so neither will this manual: Official

SPC, verbatim

"Vehicles are extremely risky in a tornado. There is no safe option when caught in a tornado in a car, just slightly less-dangerous ones."

Their options, in their priority order. Note where the ditch actually sits:

  1. Drive out of the path at right angles to the tornado's motion — if you have room, visibility, and open road. Not away from it. Across it.
  2. Get into a sturdy building or an underground shelter.
  3. Stay in the vehicle, seat belt fastened, head below the windows, covered with a blanket or coat.
  4. A ditch or culvert — and only if you can get noticeably lower than the roadway. Not a shallow shoulder.

The ditch is last, not first

Folk wisdom has "get in a ditch" as the default answer. SPC has it fourth, and there's a catch nobody mentions: ditches flood. The same storm producing the tornado is usually producing flash flooding at that exact moment. You can drown in the thing you climbed into to avoid the tornado.

The three places a chaser dies

You don't chase. But you should know the vocabulary, because you'll hear it, and because knowing why these are lethal is part of understanding storm structure at all.

TermNWS definition Official
Bear's cage "A region of storm-scale rotation, in a thunderstorm, which is wrapped in heavy precipitation."
Core punch "A penetration by a vehicle into the heavy precipitation core of a thunderstorm. Core punching is not a recommended procedure for storm spotting."
Inflow notch "A radar signature characterized by an indentation in the reflectivity pattern on the inflow side of the storm."

The bear's cage definition contains the entire danger in one line: the rotation is there and the rain has wrapped around it, so you can't see it. You can be inside a mesocyclone looking at nothing but grey. That's the standard failure mode on HP supercells — which is why HP storms are the most dangerous mode for a spotter. The same deep wrapping RFD that helps them produce is the thing that hides what they produced.

Notice that the NWS wrote the warning into the definition of core punch. That's unusual for a glossary. NWS Paducah says it plainer: "You can drive out of the rain and hail right into the tornado."

The notch is the gap between the precipitation core and the wall cloud — the best view in storm chasing, and the exact real estate the tornado occupies. The RFD can wrap around behind you and close it while you're sitting in it. That's how a clear notch becomes a bear's cage, and it can happen in under a minute.

Number 1 threat: the highway

That's NWS Paducah's heading, not a paraphrase. The thing that kills spotters and chasers most is not tornadoes — it's ordinary traffic. Hydroplaning, no visibility, stopping on the shoulder, and drivers looking at the sky instead of the road. On March 28, 2017, near Spur, Texas, two chase vehicles collided at an intersection and killed three people: Kelley Williamson, Randy Yarnall, and Corbin Lee Jaeger — who was himself a trained SKYWARN spotter. There was no tornado involved. It was an intersection.

Lightning, which will hurt you long before a tornado does

There is no safe place outside when thunderstorms are around. Not a tree, not a shed, not a dugout, not a picnic shelter, not a tent, not lying flat. The NWS abandoned the old "lightning crouch" precisely because it made people think some outdoor posture worked.

The two slogans that replaced all the old rules

"When Thunder Roars, Go Indoors."  ·  "See a Flash, Dash Inside."
If you can hear thunder, you are within striking distance. There's no counting, no distance threshold, no judgment call. Thunder itself is the trigger. Then wait 30 minutes after the last rumble before going back out.

Only two things count as shelter: a substantial fully-enclosed building with wiring and plumbing, or a hard-topped metal vehicle with the windows closed.

It is not the rubber tires

A car protects you because the metal shell carries the current around the outside and into the ground — a Faraday cage. It has nothing to do with the tires insulating you. Which is why a convertible, a fiberglass-body car, or a golf cart protects you from exactly nothing.

The "30-30 rule" — a case study in reading carefully

You'll find the 30-30 rule everywhere: shelter if the flash-to-bang count is under 30 seconds, wait 30 minutes after the last thunder. Here's the actual status, which is more interesting than either "it's the rule" or "it's been retired."

The first half is gone from every current national NWS lightning page — you no longer count seconds, because any thunder means go in. The second half, the 30 minutes, is still current guidance. But the NWS never published a document saying "we retired the 30-30 rule," so anyone who states that flatly is making it up. The honest version is the one above: half superseded, half retained. (Also: old spotter guides cite lightningsafety.noaa.gov. That domain is dead. It's weather.gov/safety/lightning now.)

Flooding

Flooding kills more Americans in most years than tornadoes do, and the overwhelming majority of those deaths are in vehicles. Turn Around, Don't Drown. Six inches of moving water can knock an adult off their feet; a foot can float a car. You cannot tell how deep it is or whether the road is still under there. Ever.

And the one that matters most

Read this before you decide you're the exception

Storm chasing kills experienced adults with degrees and decades of experience.

On May 31, 2013, in El Reno, Oklahoma, it killed Tim Samaras — one of the most careful, most respected researchers in the entire field, a man who had spent his life studying how to do this safely — along with his son Paul Samaras and his partner Carl Young. An amateur chaser, Richard Henderson, was killed as well. Eight people died in that tornado. Every one of them was in a vehicle.

It was 2.6 miles wide — the widest tornado ever recorded — with winds measured at 295 mph or more by mobile radar. It made a sudden left turn and expanded enormously, overtaking drivers who had made completely reasonable escape decisions based on where it had been going a minute earlier.

He was better at this than you will be for a very long time. He did everything right by the standards of the day, and the storm did something nobody expected. There is no skill level at which the atmosphere agrees to be fair. You spot from safety.

One more thing about El Reno, and it's a lesson about scales

It was announced as an EF5 on June 4, 2013. On August 30 it was changed to EF3. The tornado didn't get weaker — the Enhanced Fujita scale rates damage, not wind. Those 295 mph winds were over open Oklahoma field with nothing standing in them to destroy, so there was no EF5 damage to survey. If you ever catch yourself thinking "EF3, that's manageable," remember that the widest, most violent tornado ever measured is officially an EF3. The number describes what it hit, not what it was.

ACES — worth memorizing

  • Awareness — know the storm's structure and motion before you're near it
  • Communication — never operate alone; somebody always knows where you are
  • Escape routes — two of them, in different directions, at all times
  • Safe zones — know where the nearest sturdy building is

13 · Free tools for your PC

The Storm Deck in this lab reads live NWS data and runs off the flash drive with nothing installed. That's on purpose — it always works. But there is a point where you'll want the real thing: Level 2 radar, the full-resolution volume data straight off the NEXRAD dish, with every tilt and every dual-pol product. The professional tool for that costs $250 a year.

You don't need it.

Supercell Wx — the one to install

supercellwx.net · free · open source · Windows

  • Reads NEXRAD Level 2 and Level 3 — reflectivity, velocity, and the dual-pol products from Section 8 (ZDR, CC, KDP). This is the same underlying data the National Weather Service is looking at.
  • Live warnings and alerts drawn on the map.
  • Supports GRLevelX-compatible color palettes — meaning the palettes that experienced chasers have spent years tuning will load straight into it.
  • Free and open source. Not a trial, not a nag screen, not a student discount that expires.

This is the genuine free substitute for the paid radar software you'll see people using in videos. Install it, put it next to the Storm Deck, and use both: the Deck for the fast look and the alerts, Supercell Wx when you want to go down through the tilts and see what the storm is actually doing at 10,000 feet.

Learn the palette before you trust it

The moment you load a custom color palette, every number in Section 5 and Section 6 of this manual is still true but every color changed. Purple might now mean something completely different. Before you use a palette in anger, open it on a storm you already understand and check it against the scale. A color you misread confidently is worse than no radar at all.

Python, when you're ready to write your own

You've already written code in the Missions. When you want to go further and process real weather data yourself, Unidata — the same organization behind a lot of the software meteorologists actually use — publishes a free course: Introduction to Python for Atmospheric Science & Meteorology.

Here's why that matters more than it looks: the federal job standard for a meteorologist (OPM series GS-1340) requires 24 semester hours of meteorology plus physics and differential equations — and computer science is explicitly on the approved elective list. The coding isn't a side hobby you're doing instead of weather. It counts.
