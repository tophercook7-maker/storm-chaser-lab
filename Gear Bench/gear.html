THE GEAR BENCHEvery instrument, honestly — what it measures and what it can't

Here is the secret nobody tells you: a storm chaser is not a daredevil. A storm chaser is an instrument operator. Every real chaser is really a person standing next to a set of devices, and each device answers exactly one question about the air — how hot, how wet, how fast, how heavy, how it moved. The whole skill is knowing what each one measures, how it actually works, and — this is the part people skip — where each one lies to you. A tool you trust past its limits is worse than no tool at all. So on this bench we go device by device: what it measures, how it works, what a chaser does with the number, whether it's free or costs money, and where it plugs into code.

1 · The screens you already have

Before you spend a dollar, count what's on the bench already. A phone, a laptop, and the Lab's own Storm Deck are not "getting ready to have gear." They are three real instruments, and two of them are more powerful than anything a chaser could carry twenty years ago.

Live radar — the phone and the Deck Free

The single most important instrument in modern chasing is radar, and you can pull the exact same national radar feed the professionals use, for free. It comes from the government network of Doppler radars (NEXRAD — we'll open that machine up in section 6). Free ways to see it:

  • The Storm Deck in this Lab — built to show you reflectivity and velocity the way the Field Manual teaches to read them.
  • radar.weather.gov — the National Weather Service's own site. It's the source. It's a little plain, never lies to you, and costs nothing.

RadarScope — the paid one ~$10–100/yr

Almost every serious chaser runs RadarScope. It's not magic data — it's the same NEXRAD feed — but it's fast, it shows all the dual-pol products cleanly, it loops smoothly, and it overlays spotter reports and warnings. The base app is a one-time ~$10; the "Pro" tiers (~$10 and ~$100 a year) add things like lightning and the highest-resolution radar tilts.

The honest comparison

Paid apps don't get you better radar. The radar is the same public data for everyone. What you pay for is speed, polish, and convenience under pressure. When a storm is minutes from a decision, a smooth loop that never fights you is worth real money to a pro. For learning right now, free radar teaches you exactly the same skill.

Weather app vs. actual radar Free

This trips up almost everyone, so get it straight now. The weather app on a phone — the one with the little cloud icons and "70% chance of rain" — is showing you a forecast: a computer model's guess about the next few hours, updated slowly. Radar shows you what is physically in the sky right now, as echoes bouncing off actual raindrops and hail. A forecast can say "clear" while radar shows a supercell forty miles away and closing. Chasers live on radar and satellite. The cute app is for deciding whether to bring a jacket.

Battery bank & car charger ~$25–50

Yes, these count as gear. Radar, GPS, and the mapping you'll do drain a phone fast, and a dead phone in the field is a dead instrument. A ~10,000 mAh battery bank is a couple of full recharges in your pocket; a car USB charger keeps the laptop and phone alive on long drives. Boring, cheap, and the reason your other tools keep working.

Paper maps ~$0–20

This sounds old-fashioned until the day it saves you. Cell service dies exactly where storms love to be — out in open country, and again when a tornado knocks out towers and everyone floods the network at once. When the map app spins forever, a paper county map (a DeLorme Atlas & Gazetteer for your state is the classic) still shows you the roads, and roads are how you keep a safe distance and an escape route. A tool that needs a signal isn't a tool you can count on. Paper needs nothing.

2 · Radios & signals

Storms make radio interesting for two reasons: warnings arrive by radio, and chasers talk by radio. And one little radio gadget turns your laptop into a receiver you can program yourself — that's the one to get excited about.

NOAA Weather Radio ~$30

This receives a government broadcast (NWR — "the voice of the National Weather Service") on seven VHF channels around 162 MHz. It runs 24/7 with forecasts, and when a warning is issued it fires a loud alert — even at 3 a.m. with the radio "off." The reason every spotter owns one is that it doesn't depend on cell service, apps, or internet. It's a straight line from the Weather Service to your kitchen.

How SAME lets it stay quiet until it matters

SAME = Specific Area Message Encoding. Every U.S. county has a six-digit code. You program your county's codes into the radio; then it stays silent for alerts in far-off counties and only screams for the ones you chose. The alert itself carries a code for what it is (tornado warning vs. flood watch), so a good radio can even alert only for the dangerous stuff. That's the same alerting system behind the tones on TV and on phones.

Ham radio (2 m / 70 cm) ~$30 radio + license

Amateur ("ham") radio is how real spotters talk to each other and to the Weather Service during severe weather. On the 2-meter (144 MHz) and 70-centimeter (440 MHz) bands, spotters check into a SKYWARN net — an organized on-air roundtable where a net control operator collects live reports ("hail the size of quarters at Main and 5th") and relays them straight to forecasters. A cheap handheld radio (a Baofeng is ~$30) can receive right now; to legally transmit you need a Technician license, which is a real exam but very passable for a sharp 13-year-old. See the SKYWARN page for how spotting and nets fit together.

Do this

You can start today: listen. A handheld or the RTL-SDR below can receive your local 2 m repeater with no license at all. Find your nearest SKYWARN net time and just listen to how pros report. That's free training in the exact language you'll use.

CB radio ~$50–90

Citizens Band (around 27 MHz) is the old truckers' band — no license needed, short range, and mostly used by chasers to hear road and storm chatter from other vehicles nearby. It's a minor tool today; ham is where the real spotter traffic lives. Worth knowing it exists, not worth rushing to buy.

RTL-SDR dongle — the coder's favorite ~$30

Here's the one that should make a programmer grin. An RTL-SDR is a little USB stick, about $30, that turns your laptop into a software-defined radio: instead of knobs and fixed channels, software decides what frequency to tune and how to decode it. One $30 stick can:

  • Receive NOAA Weather Radio around 162 MHz — the same broadcast as the $30 dedicated radio, but on your screen.
  • Track aircraft — planes broadcast their position on 1090 MHz (ADS-B). Point free software at your dongle and watch a live map of every plane overhead.
  • Decode live weather satellite images — the older NOAA polar satellites transmit pictures in a format called APT around 137 MHz as they pass over. With a cheap antenna and free software you can pull a real cloud image straight out of the sky, from space, with no internet at all.

Why this is the perfect gear-plus-code project

The RTL-SDR is where hardware, radio physics, and Python meet. Tools like rtl_433 decode signals from wireless weather stations and sensors around your neighborhood, and Python libraries can read the dongle directly. For about $30 you own a receiver that would have cost thousands as separate machines — and you write what it does. When you're ready, the Code School is where the Python for it lives.

3 · Measuring the air

Everything a storm does starts as a difference in the air: warm against cool, wet against dry, calm against fast. Each instrument below pins down exactly one of those. Learn them one at a time and you'll start to feel a forecast, not just read one.

Thermometer — temperature ~$10–25

Measures how hot the air is. Simple, but the base of everything: warm air near the ground is the buoyant fuel a storm rises on. What matters to a chaser isn't one number — it's the difference between the warm surface air and the cold air aloft. A big difference means air wants to rise fast, and fast-rising air is a storm engine. (The Field Manual calls this "instability.")

Hygrometer & dewpoint — moisture ~$15–40

A hygrometer measures how much water vapor is in the air. But the number that actually matters is dewpoint — the temperature the air would have to cool to before water starts condensing out. Here is the thing most people get backwards:

Dewpoint is the fuel gauge, not "humidity %"

Relative humidity is a fraction — moisture compared to how much the air could hold at that temperature — so it swings around all day just because the temperature changes. It's a slippery number. Dewpoint is an absolute amount of moisture, so it tells you honestly how much fuel is in the air. Chasers watch dewpoint like a fuel gauge: dewpoints climbing into the 60s and 70s °F mean the atmosphere is loaded and ready. The Field Manual leans on dewpoint for exactly this reason — trust it over the humidity percentage.

Barometer — pressure ~$20–50

Measures the weight of the air pressing down (in millibars/hectopascals, or inches of mercury). The trend is what matters. A falling barometer means a low-pressure system — stormy weather — is moving in; a rising one means it's clearing out. Sailors and farmers read barometers for centuries before satellites for this reason. A sudden pressure drop is the atmosphere telling you a change is close.

Anemometer — wind speed ~$70–300

Measures how fast the wind blows. The classic design is three little cups on a spinner — faster wind, faster spin. Handheld models let you clock a gust on the spot. The gold standard for chasers is the Kestrel line (roughly $70 for a basic one up to $300+ for a full weather meter that also reads temp, humidity, dewpoint, and pressure — a whole weather station in your hand). Wind speed tells a spotter about outflow, gust fronts, and — near a storm — how organized the low-level winds are getting.

Wind vane — wind direction ~$15–40

Points into the wind and tells you where it's coming from. Sounds simple; it's quietly one of the most important readings a spotter takes. A wind shift — the surface wind suddenly swinging around, say from south to north — often marks a boundary or a gust front passing, and boundaries are where storms fire and intensify. Chasers watch for wind that backs (turns counterclockwise) ahead of a storm, because turning winds are a sign the air is starting to spin.

Rain gauge — precipitation ~$10–35

Measures how much rain actually fell, in inches or millimeters. Radar estimates rainfall; a rain gauge confirms it, and that ground truth is genuinely useful to forecasters — especially for flooding.

You can be a real data source

CoCoRaHS (the Community Collaborative Rain, Hail and Snow Network) is a nationwide project where regular people report their daily rain-gauge reading, and that data goes to the Weather Service and researchers. A ~$30 official gauge and five minutes a morning make you a genuine contributor. It's covered on the SKYWARN page — real science you can do this week.

Disdrometer — drop size Research

A rain gauge tells you how much fell; a disdrometer tells you the size and speed of individual drops by sensing each one that passes through a laser beam or strikes a sensor. Researchers use it to check what radar is really seeing, because big drops and small drops bounce radar differently. You won't own one, but it's why scientists trust — and correct — radar rainfall estimates.

Build a sling psychrometer — free dewpoint Free / DIY

Want dewpoint without buying a thing? A sling psychrometer is two thermometers, one with a wet cloth ("wick") over its bulb, mounted so you can spin them through the air. The wet one reads colder because evaporation cools it — and how much colder depends on how dry the air is. Dry air, lots of evaporation, big gap. Humid air, little evaporation, small gap. You look the two readings up on a chart (or compute it) to get the dewpoint. Two cheap thermometers, a shoelace, a bit of string — a real instrument you built and understand from the inside out.

4 · The chase vehicle

Read this first — no exceptions

Actually driving to chase a storm is years away for you, and it only ever happens with a trained, experienced adult who is doing the driving. This isn't a grown-up being cautious for fun. On May 31, 2013, near El Reno, Oklahoma, a tornado suddenly widened to 2.6 miles across — the widest ever recorded — and shifted direction faster than anyone expected. Experienced chasers were caught, and three of them, Tim Samaras, Paul Samaras, and Carl Young, were killed. These were among the most careful, most respected researchers in the field. The storm didn't care. You do not chase alone, you do not chase to drive fast, and the road is a tool for keeping distance and an escape route — never for getting close. The Missions safety rules are not softened, and neither is this.

With that fixed in place, here's what actually rides in a chase vehicle — because understanding the gear now is how you earn the seat later.

Dash cam ~$50–200

A camera on the windshield that records the whole drive. It documents what a storm did, catches structure and hazards, and — honestly — settles arguments and provides evidence after the fact. Simple, useful, no downside.

Laptop mount & mobile hotspot ~$30 mount + data plan

A sturdy mount keeps the radar laptop readable for the navigator (never the driver — the driver drives, period). A mobile hotspot or a phone's hotspot keeps radar and mapping flowing where there's coverage. Note the built-in limit: this dies exactly where storms roam, which is why paper maps live in the door pocket.

GPS ~$100–250 or free on phone

Knowing precisely where you are — and where the roads go — is a safety instrument, not a convenience, when a storm is nearby. A dedicated GPS keeps working when cell data doesn't. Phone GPS is free and excellent until you lose signal.

Power inverter & battery ~$30–120

An inverter turns the car's 12 V into household power so laptops and radios keep running all day. A second deep-cycle battery or a big power station means the electronics don't drain the battery you need to start the car and leave — which, near a storm, is the whole point.

Ham radio rig ~$150–400

A mobile 2 m / 70 cm radio with a mag-mount antenna on the roof — the vehicle version of the handheld from section 2. This is how the chase team stays on the SKYWARN net and hears other spotters when cell service is gone.

Hail guards ~$40–300

Supercells throw hail the size of golf balls, baseballs, sometimes grapefruit, and it will smash a windshield. Chasers use mesh screens, portable "hail nets," or simply position the car to shield the glass. It's about protecting the vehicle you're depending on to get you out.

Mobile mesonet Research

This is the pro's crown jewel and worth understanding. A mobile mesonet is a rack of research instruments — temperature, dewpoint, pressure, wind — mounted on the roof, feeding a computer that logs a reading every second, tagged with GPS position. Because the vehicle is moving, scientists can drive a transect through and around a storm and sample its structure across space and time — for example, mapping exactly where the cold "rear-flank downdraft" air wraps around a tornado. It turns the car itself into a moving weather station, and it's how a lot of what we know about tornadoes was actually measured.

5 · Build your own weather station

This is where the Gear Bench and the Code School shake hands. You don't have to buy most of the instruments in section 3 — you can build them, wire them into a little computer, and write the code that reads them. For about $150–250 you can have a real, working weather station of your own design. Here's the map.

Raspberry Pi vs. Arduino ~$15–80

These are the two little brains, and they're good at different jobs:

A common setup: an Arduino reads the sensors and hands the numbers to a Raspberry Pi, and the Pi logs them and shows a web dashboard. But a Pi alone can do the whole job for a beginner, and it lets you stay in Python — which is why we'll lean on it.

The BME280 — three instruments on one chip ~$5

The single best five dollars on this whole page. The BME280 is a fingernail-sized sensor that measures temperature, pressure, and humidity all at once, and reports them over a two-wire connection called I²C. Four wires to a Raspberry Pi (power, ground, and the two I²C lines) and a few lines of Python, and you're reading the real atmosphere:

# reading a BME280 on a Raspberry Pi
import board, adafruit_bme280.advanced as bme

sensor = bme.Adafruit_BME280_I2C(board.I2C())

print(f"Temp:     {sensor.temperature:.1f} °C")
print(f"Humidity: {sensor.relative_humidity:.0f} %")
print(f"Pressure: {sensor.pressure:.1f} hPa")

That's it. Three of the instruments from section 3, live, in numbers your code owns. From here it's a short hop to computing dewpoint from temperature and humidity yourself.

DIY anemometer — counting spins ~$10 in parts

Wind speed is beautifully simple to build. Put a small magnet on a spinning cup assembly and a reed switch (a switch that clicks shut when a magnet passes) beside it. Every spin, the magnet passes the switch and makes one electrical pulse. Your code just counts pulses per second — more pulses, faster wind — and multiplies by a number you find by calibration (drive it past a known wind speed, or check it against a real anemometer once). The idea in code:

# count reed-switch pulses for a few seconds -> wind speed
pulses = 0
def on_spin(pin):          # called each time the magnet passes
    global pulses
    pulses += 1

# ...run for 5 seconds, then:
wind_mph = (pulses / 5) * CALIBRATION   # CALIBRATION found by testing

Tipping-bucket rain gauge ~$15–30

The clever standard design: rain funnels into a tiny two-sided see-saw bucket. When one side fills with a set amount of water (often 0.01 inch), it tips, dumps, and — like the anemometer — trips a switch once. Your code counts tips and multiplies: rain = tips * 0.01 inches. Same trick as the anemometer: turn a physical event into a countable pulse and let the code do the math.

Wire it into a dashboard — the payoff ~$150–250 total

Now the fun part. Read all your sensors on a loop, write each reading to a file or a small database with a timestamp, and draw it. Suddenly you have your own weather station logging temperature, pressure, humidity, wind, and rain — plotting the last 24 hours, catching the barometer falling before a storm, and it's yours because you built every piece and wrote every line.

The grown-up libraries

When you're ready to level up, the real meteorology toolkit is Python too. MetPy does actual weather math and draws Skew-T soundings; Siphon downloads real observation and model data from the same servers professionals use. They're the same tools working scientists run. Start with your $5 sensor; grow into the tools the pros use, in the same language. The Code School is where you'll pick up the Python to drive all of this.

6 · The big machines

These are the instruments you'll (almost) never touch, but every free tool you use is built on their data. Knowing how they work is knowing how the pros actually see the atmosphere. This is the research tier — aspirational, and worth understanding cold.

NEXRAD — the national Doppler radar Research

The network of WSR-88D radars (about 160 across the U.S.) is the source of every radar image you've ever looked at. Each one sweeps a beam of microwaves around the sky; the beam bounces off raindrops, hail, and even bugs, and the radar listens for the echo. It reads three different things from that echo:

  • Reflectivity — how strong the echo is, which tells you how heavy the precipitation is. This is the classic green-to-red rain map.
  • Velocity — using the Doppler effect (echoes shift frequency if the target is moving toward or away), it measures whether the rain is coming or going. Rain coming and going right next to each other means the air is spinning — the fingerprint of a possible tornado.
  • Dual-polarization — the radar sends pulses both horizontally and vertically, and comparing them reveals the shape of what's up there: round raindrops vs. jagged hail vs. debris lofted by a tornado (the famous "debris ball").

The Field Manual teaches you to read reflectivity and velocity by eye. This is the machine making those pictures.

Radiosonde / weather balloon Research

Twice a day, at the same moment worldwide (00 and 12 UTC), weather offices release balloons carrying a small instrument package called a radiosonde. As the balloon rises to ~100,000 feet and pops, the sonde radios back temperature, humidity, pressure, and wind at every height. That vertical slice is plotted on a chart called a Skew-T.

The single most important chart in forecasting

A Skew-T shows temperature and dewpoint from the ground straight up. From it a forecaster reads whether the air will rise (instability), how much fuel is up there, whether a "cap" is holding storms down, and how the winds turn with height (whether storms can spin). More than any radar image, the Skew-T is where a chaser decides whether to even leave the house. It's worth learning to read — and MetPy from section 5 will draw one for you.

Doppler on Wheels (DOW) Research

A full NEXRAD-style Doppler radar shrunk down and mounted on a truck. Because it can drive to within a mile or two of a tornado, a DOW scans the storm at far higher resolution than a fixed radar hundreds of miles away — close enough to measure the winds inside a tornado. It's how researchers got the highest tornado wind speeds ever recorded, over 300 mph in a 1999 Oklahoma tornado.

Lightning detection Research

Two systems, working together. On the ground, networks of sensors (like the National Lightning Detection Network) triangulate each strike by the exact timing of the radio "crack" it makes, pinpointing where and when it hit. From above, the GLM (Geostationary Lightning Mapper) rides on the GOES satellites and watches whole storms flash from space, day and night. A sudden burst of lightning ("a lightning jump") often comes just before a storm turns severe.

Weather satellites — GOES Research

The big picture comes from the GOES satellites (the newest is GOES-19). They sit in geostationary orbit — about 22,236 miles up, circling at exactly the speed Earth turns, so each one hovers over the same spot forever and can film the same half of the planet continuously. They watch in several channels:

  • Visible — sunlight reflecting off clouds; sharp and detailed, but only works in daylight.
  • Infrared — heat, so it works at night; colder cloud tops glow differently, and colder tops mean taller, more powerful storms.
  • Water vapor — sees invisible moisture high in the atmosphere, revealing the flow and dry slots that steer and feed storms.

And remember section 2: with a $30 RTL-SDR and an antenna, you can pull a real satellite image out of the sky yourself. The pros' data and your bench are closer than they look.

The one instrument that matters most

Every device on this bench measures one thing about the air. Not one of them decides whether it's safe, whether a storm is doing what you expected, or whether it's time to leave. That's the trained brain — and its most important habit is writing down what you actually see. A reading you wrote down beats a memory every time, and it's how you get better. That's the whole point of the Chase Log. No device replaces the person reading it. Build the gear. Then build the operator.
