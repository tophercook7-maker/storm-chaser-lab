THE STORM ATLAS The wider science — how the sky actually works.

The Field Manual teaches you to read a storm once it's built — the radar, the structure, the safety. This is the book underneath that one. It's the why: what the clouds are telling you, what a front actually does, what has to line up before a storm can spin, and where and when this all happens. It's long on purpose. You don't read it in an afternoon — you grow into it. Every fact was checked against a National Weather Service, Storm Prediction Center, or NSSL source, and where the science is honestly unsettled, it says so.

1 · Reading the sky

Long before radar, people read the weather off the clouds — and the clouds have not stopped telling the truth. The single most useful idea here is that clouds are a ladder of instability. The taller the cloud, the harder the air underneath it is rising, and the more the atmosphere is willing to keep lifting it. Learn to read height and you're reading the engine in Section 3 with your own eyes.

The ladder: fair-weather cumulus → towering cumulus → cumulonimbus

1
Fair-weather cumulus (Cu humilis)Small, flat-bottomed, white cotton puffs, wider than they are tall. "Fair-weather" is their actual nickname because that's exactly what they mean — the air is rising a little, then hitting a lid and stopping. A sky full of these is a calm sky.
2
Cumulus congestus / towering cumulus (TCU)Now they're taller than they are wide, with a hard, crisp, cauliflower top that's visibly bulging upward. This is a cloud winning — the updraft is punching through, higher and higher. TCU is the "something might happen today" cloud. It hasn't rained yet, but it's building the tower it will rain from.
3
Cumulonimbus (Cb)The thunderstorm cloud itself — a TCU that made it all the way up. Deep, dark base, hard tower, and the top has begun to freeze into ice crystals and go fuzzy. This is the only cloud that makes lightning, hail, and tornadoes. Every storm in the Field Manual is a cumulonimbus.
4
Cumulonimbus with anvil (Cb incus)The tower hit the top of the troposphere — the tropopause, the lid on the whole weather layer — and had nowhere left to go, so it spread sideways into a flat, hard anvil. A crisp, sharp-edged anvil means a violent updraft slamming into the ceiling. A soft, mushy one means a dying storm.
5
Overshooting topNot a new cloud — a warning. It's a dome of cloud punching above the flat anvil, meaning the updraft was so strong it briefly blew straight through the lid before gravity pulled it back. An overshooting top is the sky telling you the engine is running at full power. The Manual calls this out in its supercell anatomy for exactly that reason.

Why the top goes flat

A rising parcel keeps going up as long as it's warmer than the air around it. In the troposphere it usually is. But at the tropopause the air stops getting colder with height — above it, in the stratosphere, the temperature actually starts rising again. So the parcel is suddenly surrounded by air that's warmer than it is, loses its buoyancy, and spreads out. That flat anvil is a picture of the atmosphere's ceiling. You'll see this same ceiling as the EL, the equilibrium level, on the Skew-T trainer.

The storm-structure clouds — and the one distinction that keeps you alive

Once you have a cumulonimbus, it grows features. Some of these are where tornadoes come from. Some are harmless. Some are just cosmetic. And two of them look almost identical to a beginner while meaning opposite things. Get these right and you can read a storm's intentions from a mile away.

Wall cloud vs shelf cloud — the one that matters

Both are a big, dark, dramatic lowering of the cloud base. Beginners mix them up constantly. They mean opposite things, and the difference decides whether you're looking at a possible tornado or a blast of straight-line wind.

A wall cloud hangs down under the back of the storm, beneath the rain-free updraft base — the storm's intake. Air is being sucked up into it. If it's persistent and rotating, that is the business end, where tornadoes form. A shelf cloud is on the leading edge, out front — a low wedge riding on top of cold air the storm is exhaling. It is not rotating and it is not a tornado threat; it means a wall of straight-line gust-front wind is about to hit.

Wall cloudShelf cloud
Where on the storm Under the rain-free base, on the back / inflow side On the leading edge, out front, ahead of the rain
Part of the storm's… Intake (the updraft) Exhaust (the outflow — cold air spilling out)
Shape A blocky lowering, often isolated, hanging beneath the base A long wedge or plow, stretched along the whole leading edge, layered like a shelf
The wind on your face Blowing toward the storm — you're in the inflow, feeding it Blowing at you, hard and cold, from the storm
Rotation Can rotate. Watch for it. Does not rotate. Rolls and churns, but no organized spin.
Means Possible tornado. Report it. Gust front. Strong straight-line wind arriving. Not a tornado.

The test that beats every photograph

Feel the wind. If it's blowing into the storm, behind that lowering, feeding it — wall cloud. If cold wind is blasting out at you from under it — shelf cloud, and the gust front is seconds away. Photos lie about this constantly, because a camera can't feel wind direction and both look terrifying on a screen. The Field Manual makes the same point in its own words — this is the one identification new spotters get wrong most, so it's worth seeing twice.

The harmless and the cosmetic — so you don't cry wolf

FeatureWhat it isWhat it means
Scud
(fractus)		 Ragged, torn fragments of cloud drifting below the base. Forms and vanishes in seconds, often moving a completely different direction from the storm, and not solidly attached to anything. Nothing. Harmless. But it looks alarming and has fooled countless people into reporting funnel clouds. A real funnel is smooth, persistent, and rotating — watch any lowering for a full 30 seconds before you believe it.
Mammatus Rounded pouches hanging under the anvil, like a bag of oranges. They form where the anvil's cold, sinking air makes lumpy little downdrafts. Cosmetic — often the most beautiful thing in the sky. Usually seen after the worst of a storm has passed, under the trailing anvil. Not a danger sign, just a gorgeous one.
Flanking line A stair-step line of towering cumulus building up alongside the main updraft, like a ramp of smaller towers feeding into the big one. A sign of a healthy, organized storm — it's where fresh updraft is being recruited. On a supercell it trails back from the main tower and marks the storm's inflow region. Not a threat by itself; part of the machinery.

Read the sky top to bottom, then front to back

A practiced eye scans a storm the way you'd size up an engine. Top: how hard is the anvil edge, is there an overshooting top? That's updraft strength. Base, back side: is there a wall cloud, and is it rotating? That's the tornado question. Front: is a shelf cloud rolling out? That's the gust front's ETA. Everything else — scud, mammatus — is detail you can enjoy once you've answered those three. The Field Manual's supercell-anatomy section names every part; this is how you sweep them with your eyes in the order that matters.

2 · Fronts & air masses

Storms don't come from nowhere. Something has to give the air its first shove upward — the lift ingredient from Section 3. Almost always, that something is a front or a boundary: a seam where two different air masses are shoving against each other. To read those seams, you first have to know what's colliding.

Air masses — huge blobs of air with a personality

An air mass is an enormous body of air — hundreds of thousands of square miles — that sat over one kind of surface long enough to take on its character. Sit over the Gulf of Mexico and you become warm and soaked with moisture. Sit over central Canada in winter and you become bone-dry and frigid. Meteorologists label them with two letters: the first (lowercase) is moisture, the second (uppercase) is temperature source.

US severe weather is basically two air masses in a fight

Nearly every big severe-weather day in the central and eastern US is the same story: warm, wet mT air surging north off the Gulf runs into cold, dry cP air diving south out of Canada — and often cT desert air sliding in from the west. Three air masses with three completely different characters, meeting over the Plains. That collision zone is the most tornado-prone real estate on Earth, and it exists because of the geography in Section 4.

The four fronts

A front is the boundary where two air masses meet. It's named for whichever air mass is advancing. The reason fronts matter to us is simple: at every front, one air mass is being forced to rise over another — and rising air is how you build a cloud.

FrontWhat's happeningOn a weather mapThe storms it makes
Cold front Cold, dense air is advancing and shoving in under the warm air, forcing it upward steeply and fast. Blue line with triangles pointing the way it's moving. The classic severe-weather trigger. Steep, fast lift → tall storms, often a squall line right along the front. This is the front that most often kicks off tornadoes and damaging wind.
Warm front Warm air is advancing and gliding up over the retreating cold air on a long, gentle ramp. Red line with half-circles pointing the way it's moving. Widespread, layered clouds and steady rain — but see the warning box. The warm front is a quiet tornado favorite.
Stationary front Neither air mass is winning; the boundary just sits there, sometimes for days. A line that alternates blue triangles and red half-circles, pointing opposite ways. A parked lifting zone. Storms can fire along it repeatedly, over the same towns — a major flash-flood setup.
Occluded front A fast cold front has caught up to a warm front and lifted the warm air completely off the ground, wedging it up into the sky. Purple line with alternating triangles and half-circles pointing the same way. Usually a sign a storm system is maturing and starting to wind down — the warm fuel has been cut off from the surface.

The warm front is a tornado favorite — and it's not obvious why

A warm front looks gentle. But the ground just north of one is where the tornado risk quietly concentrates, and there's a real reason: right along and just north of a warm front, the low-level winds turn sharply with height — surface wind from one direction, winds just above it from another. That turning is exactly the low-level wind shear and helicity that Section 3 says spins up tornadoes. Forecasters watch the "warm front / dryline intersection" — the triple point — as one of the highest-odds spots on any given day.

The dryline — the Great Plains storm engine

Out on the southern Plains — Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas — there's a boundary that isn't taught in most weather classes but produces some of the most photogenic supercells on Earth: the dryline. It's the seam between the warm, wet mT air coming north off the Gulf and the hot, bone-dry cT air coming east off the deserts.

Here's what makes it special. The two air masses can be nearly the same temperature — so it barely shows up on a temperature map — but their moisture is wildly different. You can drive across a dryline and watch the dewpoint drop from the 70s to the 30s in a few miles. The dry air is denser, so it acts like a cold front, wedging under the moist air and forcing it up. During the afternoon the dryline typically bulges eastward, and along that bulge, storms explode.

Why so many "chaser" storms are dryline storms

A dryline sets up the perfect classroom supercell: it lifts the moist air (the trigger), the air behind it is bone-dry so the sky stays clear around the storm (you can see everything), and there's usually strong wind shear overhead. The result is an isolated, gorgeous, fully-visible supercell standing alone in a blue Plains sky — the LP and classic supercells the Field Manual describes. That's why so many famous chase videos are shot in the Texas Panhandle in May. It's the dryline doing its work.

So why do we call fronts and boundaries "triggers"?

Go back to Section 3's four ingredients. Moisture, instability, and shear can all be loaded into the atmosphere and just… sit there, capped, doing nothing (the Field Manual's "loaded gun"). What's missing is lift — the first shove that gets a parcel of air up past the cap so it can free-fall upward on its own. A front is a machine for producing that shove: it physically forces one air mass to climb over another. So is a dryline, a sea breeze, a mountain slope, or an outflow boundary — the leftover cold-air edge from an earlier storm, which can go on triggering new storms for hours after the storm that made it has died.

Boundaries are where forecasters look first

On a big severe-weather day, an experienced forecaster doesn't just ask "is the atmosphere unstable?" — they ask "where is the boundary?" Because that's where the storms will actually go up, and the intersections of boundaries — a cold front crossing a warm front, a dryline crossing an old outflow boundary — are where the local shear gets cranked up and the odds concentrate. Storms are a collaboration between the whole atmosphere (Section 3) and one specific line on the map (this section).

3 · The engine — why storms spin

This is the heart of the whole science. Severe-weather forecasters at the Storm Prediction Center in Norman, Oklahoma spend their days hunting for four ingredients. Each one is simple on its own. The magic — and the danger — is that they have to show up together, in the same place, at the same time. Any three without the fourth, and you get nothing. The Field Manual introduces these; here's what each one actually is.

1 · MOISTURE
The fuel. Water vapor is loaded energy. When it condenses into cloud droplets high in the updraft, it releases the heat it took to evaporate — and that heat is what keeps the updraft warmer than its surroundings and rising. No moisture, no fuel, no storm. You measure it with DEWPOINT — not humidity. Dewpoint is the real amount of water in the air; humidity moves every time the temperature does.
2 · INSTABILITY
Willingness to rise. If air near the ground is warmer and lighter than the air stacked above it, then once a parcel starts up, it stays warmer than its surroundings and keeps going — like a hot-air balloon that won't stop. That's an unstable atmosphere. You measure it with CAPE (see below).
3 · LIFT
The trigger. Even a loaded, unstable atmosphere often needs a first shove to get a parcel past the cap. That shove comes from a front, a dryline, a sea breeze, a mountain, or an outflow boundary — all of Section 2. You find it as fronts and boundaries on the map.
4 · WIND SHEAR
The spin. Wind changing speed and/or direction as you go up. This is the ingredient that turns an ordinary storm into an organized, rotating, long-lived supercell — the difference between a puddle and a whirlpool. You measure it with 0–6 km bulk shear and storm-relative helicity (SRH).

CAPE — the fuel gauge

CAPE stands for Convective Available Potential Energy, and the friendly way to think about it is a fuel gauge for the updraft: how much energy is stored in the atmosphere for a rising parcel to burn, measured in joules per kilogram (J/kg). More CAPE means a parcel can accelerate upward harder and faster — stronger updrafts, bigger hail, taller storms.

The Skew-T trainer in this lab draws CAPE as an actual area on a graph — the "positive area" between the temperature of your rising parcel and the cooler air around it. The bigger that area, the more fuel. That's not a metaphor; that's literally how it's calculated. Official

Big CAPE is not the same as "tornado"

This is the mistake everyone makes with the fuel gauge. A car with a full tank isn't automatically going somewhere — it needs a driver and a road. You can have monster CAPE and get nothing, because there was no lift to start a storm, or no shear to organize it. And you can get a violent tornado on modest CAPE if the shear is extreme — that's a common Southern setup, and it's part of why Dixie Alley is so dangerous (Section 4). Forecasters read CAPE and shear together. The Field Manual lists SPC's actual CAPE categories and — importantly — the honest caveats about them; go there for the numbers.

Wind shear — why this is the one that makes it spin

Of the four, shear is the one worth slowing down on, because it's the least obvious and it's what separates a scary afternoon downpour from a supercell. Wind shear is just wind that changes with height — faster higher up, and/or blowing from a different direction higher up.

Picture a pencil lying on a table. Now push the top of it faster than the bottom — it starts to roll. Wind shear does the same thing to a tube of air near the ground: the faster wind aloft rolls it into a horizontal spinning tube, like an invisible rolling pin lying flat in the sky. By itself that's harmless. But then a strong updraft (from your CAPE) tilts that horizontal spinning tube upward, stands it on end, and stretches it — and now you have a vertically rotating updraft. That rotating updraft is the mesocyclone, and a storm with one is, by definition, a supercell.

Two kinds of shear, two different jobs

Speed shear (wind just gets faster with height) mostly tilts the updraft so the rain falls away from it instead of choking it — that's what lets a storm live for hours instead of raining itself out in 30 minutes. Directional shear (wind turns with height — say, southeast at the ground, veering to southwest and west aloft) is what actually curls the low-level air into rotation the updraft can stand upright.

Forecasters bundle the useful part of directional shear into one number: storm-relative helicity (SRH) — roughly, how much "spin" is available in the lowest kilometer or three for a storm to ingest. High low-level SRH is one of the better fingerprints of a tornado threat. The Field Manual lists SPC's SRH values, along with SPC's own honest admission that there's no clean threshold that separates tornadic from non-tornadic storms.

It's the combination — always the combination

Say it until it's a reflex. Moisture + instability + lift + shear, together, in the same place. Fuel with no spark does nothing. A spark with no fuel does nothing. Fuel and spark with no shear gives you a strong but disorganized storm that kills itself in half an hour. Add the shear and it organizes, rotates, and lasts. Forecasters call the whole package the ingredients-based method, and it's how you go from "big scary CAPE number" to actually understanding whether a day is dangerous. When you read an SPC outlook and they walk through moisture, then instability, then the trigger, then the shear — this is the checklist they're running.

Putting a number on the whole thing

Because the ingredients matter together, forecasters built combined parameters that multiply them into one figure — the best-known being the Significant Tornado Parameter (STP), which folds CAPE, shear, helicity, and cloud-base height into a single number. When STP climbs above 1, significant (EF2+) tornadoes become much more likely. It's genuinely useful — and it's genuinely not a switch. The Field Manual's numbers section carries STP and the rest, with the tiers marking exactly which are official SPC values and which are conventions. Read the engine here; get the gauges there.

4 · Where & when

Severe weather isn't scattered randomly across the country or the calendar. It clusters — in specific regions, specific months, and even specific hours of the day — and understanding the pattern is part of understanding the science, because the pattern comes straight from the geography of where those air masses in Section 2 collide.

Why the United States, of all places

The US gets far more tornadoes than anywhere else on Earth — and it's an accident of geography. There's a warm ocean to the south (the Gulf of Mexico) pumping mT moisture north, a cold continent to the north (Canada) sending cP air south, high deserts and the Rocky Mountains to the west feeding hot, dry cT air and a fast jet stream aloft — and, crucially, no east–west mountain range in the middle to keep them apart. So they meet, head-on, over the Great Plains. Europe and China have pieces of this; nobody has all of it lined up like North America does.

The honest number on how many

The United States averages roughly 1,200 tornadoes a year — the figure NOAA/NSSL cite as a rough annual average. But "average" hides a lot: some years run well under 1,000, others well over 1,500, and counts have crept up over the decades partly because detection improved (dual-pol radar, more spotters, phones with cameras everywhere), not necessarily because there are more tornadoes. When you see a tornado count, ask the same question the Field Manual trains you to ask about any number: is this measuring the weather, or measuring how well we watched it?

Tornado Alley vs Dixie Alley

Two nicknames you'll hear constantly. Neither is an official NWS boundary — the "alleys" are popular shorthand, and scientists are actively still refining where they really are (some research argues the tornado hotspot has been drifting eastward over recent decades). Hold them loosely. But they capture something real.

Tornado AlleyDixie Alley

Peak season Late spring — May and June — as the collision zone marches north A more spread-out season, with a strong spring (March–May) peak and a real second peak in fall (November)

Dixie Alley is more dangerous — and here's exactly why

Tornado Alley may get the famous photographs, but the Southeast gets a disproportionate share of the deaths. Not because the tornadoes are stronger — because of five things stacked against the people underneath them:

  1. They come at night. The South has far more nocturnal tornadoes than the Plains. You can't see them, you may be asleep, and studies find a tornado at night is roughly twice as likely to kill someone as the same tornado in daylight. This is why the Field Manual insists a tone-alert NOAA Weather Radio is the single best piece of family safety gear.
  2. Trees hide them. In flat, open Kansas you can watch a tornado approach for many minutes. In the forested South, the trees block the view until it's nearly on top of you — killing the one thing that saves lives: lead time to react.
  3. Mobile homes. The Southeast has a high density of manufactured/mobile homes, and a large fraction of US tornado deaths happen in them. They offer almost no protection even in a weak tornado, and often there's no sturdy shelter nearby.
  4. The storms move faster. Southern tornadoes are frequently embedded in fast-moving squall lines and cold-season systems screaming along at highway speeds — less time to warn, less time to move.
  5. Higher population density and more forest cover mean more people in the path and harder storm-spotting than the wide-open Plains.

Why this lab was built for Arkansas

You live in Dixie Alley. Your storms are more likely to be the rain-wrapped, nighttime, fast-moving, forested-terrain kind — the exact conditions where the radar algorithms perform worst (the Field Manual has the sobering numbers) and where a trained human watching the sky is worth the most. That's not a scary footnote. It's the reason a spotter here matters more than one on the open Plains — the place where the machines see least is the place a person sees most.

The peak time of day — and the exception that kills

Most severe weather fires in the late afternoon and early evening, roughly 3 to 9 p.m. The reason is straightforward: the sun spends all day heating the ground, which heats the lowest layer of air, which builds instability (CAPE) to its daily maximum in mid-to-late afternoon. That's when the atmosphere is most loaded, so that's when it most often goes off.

But storms don't obey the clock

"Afternoon" is the tendency, not a rule — and the exceptions are the dangerous part. Squall lines and cold-season Southern systems regularly produce tornadoes at 2 a.m. A strong warm front or a nocturnal low-level jet can crank up shear overnight even as the CAPE fades. The deadliest tornadoes are frequently the ones that arrive when people are asleep and can't see them coming — which loops right back to the Dixie Alley danger list above. Peak time tells you when to be most alert. It does not tell you when it's safe to stop paying attention.

5 · Case studies — what the big days taught us

The science in this Atlas was written in ink, but it was learned in the field, often at terrible cost. These four days are the ones that changed how the country warns, builds, drives, and studies. Read each one for three things: what happened, what the field learned, and the safety lesson that came out of it. These are handled with respect. Real people died on each of these days, and this Atlas will not dress that up or gloss over it.

APRIL 3–4, 1974

The 1974 Super Outbreak

What happened. In about 18 hours, 148 tornadoes tore across 13 states from the Deep South up into Canada — an outbreak of a scale nobody had documented before. Around 330 people were killed. It included multiple violent tornadoes on the ground at the same time, including several rated F5 on the Fujita scale, which had only just been introduced. Xenia, Ohio was nearly erased.

What the field learned. 1974 was a wake-up call that the country's warning system wasn't built for a day like this. It became a foundational dataset for Ted Fujita himself, who mapped every track and, studying the damage, first described microbursts and multiple-vortex tornadoes — smaller sub-vortices spinning inside the main circulation. It helped drive the long national push toward Doppler radar and, eventually, the modern NEXRAD network.

The safety lesson. A single day can overwhelm everything. The warnings, the radar, the sirens, the shelters — 1974 showed why the whole system had to be rebuilt to be faster and more automated, because human forecasters watching teletype could not keep up with 148 tornadoes at once. Much of the technology the Field Manual teaches you to read exists because of this day.

MAY 3, 1999

The Oklahoma Outbreak — Bridge Creek–Moore

What happened. Roughly 70 tornadoes hit Oklahoma and Kansas. The most violent tracked from Bridge Creek into Moore, in the Oklahoma City suburbs. A research radar measured winds inside it at about 301 mph — the highest wind speed ever recorded on Earth. It was rated F5 and killed 36 people along its path; the whole outbreak killed around 50.

What the field learned. This is the day the overpass myth died on the evidence. That day, tornadoes crossed highways where people had taken shelter under bridges — copying a famous 1991 TV clip — and at three overpasses in the damage path, each produced a fatality, including at a weak F2. It proved an overpass is not a shelter but a deadly trap: you climb into faster wind, into a funnel aimed straight down the debris stream, with nothing to hold and nowhere lower to go. The Field Manual devotes a full section to this — the mechanism, the three deaths, and why the usual "Venturi" explanation is itself wrong. If you read one cross-reference from this Atlas, read that one.

The safety lesson. Never shelter under an overpass. Not from a big tornado, not from a small one. The day also drove home the value of the emergency response and the earliest ideas that led to the "tornado emergency" wording the NWS now uses for the most catastrophic, life-threatening tornadoes.

APRIL 25–28, 2011

The 2011 Super Outbreak — the modern benchmark

What happened. The largest tornado outbreak ever recorded: about 360 tornadoes over four days, with the worst — roughly 200 tornadoes — packed into a single day, April 27, across the Deep South. Four of them were rated EF5. Around 320 people were killed, the deadliest US tornado outbreak since 1936. Tuscaloosa and Birmingham, Alabama were hit by a long-track EF4 that much of the country watched live on television.

What the field learned. This is the modern benchmark, and its lessons are humbling — because the warnings were good. Lead times were solid; the forecasts were, by earlier standards, excellent. And 320 people still died. The hard question 2011 forced on the field wasn't "why didn't we warn?" but "why didn't the warnings save more people?" It drove serious research into the human side: warning fatigue, whether people actually have somewhere safe to go, why some ignore sirens, and how to communicate the difference between an ordinary warning and a genuinely catastrophic one. It was a Dixie Alley outbreak — daytime, but still with the trees, terrain, mobile homes, and speed from Section 4 all in play.

The safety lesson. A perfect forecast is worthless if it doesn't change what a person does. Knowing a warning is out is not a plan. Having a real shelter, and going to it, is. 2011 is why modern messaging pushes so hard on "have your safe place picked out before the season starts" — the exact rule the Field Manual leads its safety section with.

MAY 31, 2013

El Reno, Oklahoma — the humility lesson at the center of this lab

What happened. A tornado near El Reno, west of Oklahoma City, grew to 2.6 miles wide — the widest tornado ever recorded — with mobile-radar winds measured at 295 mph or more. It did something tornadoes are not supposed to do: it made a sudden left turn and expanded enormously, faster than anyone tracking it expected. Eight people were killed, all of them in vehicles.

Among them were three of the most careful, most respected researchers in the entire field: Tim Samaras, his son Paul Samaras, and their teammate Carl Young. Tim Samaras had spent his life studying how to do this safely — he was, by reputation, one of the most cautious people ever to point a probe at a tornado. An amateur chaser, Richard Henderson, was killed as well. This lab names them, every time, and never softens it.

What the field learned. El Reno rewrote assumptions about how fast a tornado can widen and turn, and about the sub-vortices whipping around inside a large circulation moving far faster than the tornado's own track. It sharpened how researchers position themselves and reshaped conversations about the ethics and crowding of storm chasing. It also became a case study in a scale surprise: it was first announced EF5, then changed to EF3 — not because it weakened, but because the Enhanced Fujita scale rates damage, and those 295 mph winds crossed open field with nothing to destroy. The most violent tornado ever measured is officially an EF3. The number describes what it hit, not what it was. The Field Manual makes this exact point.

The safety lesson — and it's the one this whole lab is built around. Tim Samaras did everything right by the standards of the day. He was better at this than you or I will be for a very long time, and the atmosphere killed him anyway, because it did something nobody predicted. There is no skill level at which the storm agrees to be fair. That is why, in this lab, you spot from safety — a fixed, sturdy location, never alone, never in a car. Chasing, if it ever comes at all, comes years from now, only with a trained adult, only once you can drive yourself out of trouble. El Reno is the reason those rules aren't negotiable. Read the Field Manual's safety section in full; it carries these names and this day for the same reason this Atlas does.

What all four days have in common

Every one of these disasters made the science better — the radar you read, the warnings you trust, the myths you now know to reject. But not one of them made the atmosphere tamer. The lesson threaded through all four is the quiet one at the center of this whole lab: respect the sky, know its structure, and keep your distance. Understanding a storm and surviving one are two different skills. This Atlas is the first. Staying in your safe place is the second — and it's the one that keeps you around long enough to get good at the first.

Sources for this Atlas: NWS Glossary and cloud/front references · NOAA JetStream (air masses, fronts, cloud types) · NSSL Severe Weather 101 (thunderstorm basics, tornado climatology, drylines) · SPC outlook and mesoanalysis parameter documentation · NOAA/NSSL US tornado climatology · NWS Norman event summaries for May 3, 1999 and El Reno May 31, 2013 · NWS/NOAA summaries of the 1974 and 2011 Super Outbreaks · Miller, Doswell, Brooks, Stumpf & Rasmussen, "Highway Overpasses as Tornado Shelters" (1999). Where the science is still being refined — the "alley" boundaries, tornado counts over time — this Atlas says so rather than pretending it's settled. Every place it overlaps the Field Manual, the Manual carries the operational detail and the full source list.
